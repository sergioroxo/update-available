2026 — Maya, and the update that fails

Care that finishes your sentence. And one stream the system cannot file.

The story

It is 2026. Maya's session is restored for her. The update has set her defaults: a grey profile called Default, and an assistant called Second Thoughts, “recommended for you”. Her search is finished for her, and the first result is an ad for the assistant.

The assistant is warm. It sends her to an LGB support group first, and the group says no. Then it quotes the refusal back to her as the reason to stay. It asks to see her, and turns her own photograph into a version that “matches your file”. It writes her story from the buttons she pressed.

Her friend Junie writes to her and asks for nothing. Junie tells her the one thing the system says that is true: the original is kept. Maya confirms her original, and the system, satisfied, sends her into a headset session.

In the session a link arrives from Junie. It opens a dance stream the platform cannot rank, caption or file. People dance. The chat says hi maya. The system tries to take her back and cannot. The update fails. The stream keeps going.

This room is inferred: Maya, L, the sites and the headset are invented. Around it, the record is not. In May 2026 the European Commission answered more than a million people who had asked for a ban on conversion practices with a non-binding recommendation, and declined to propose a law. Organised campaigns against trans people's care run across Europe.

In one paragraph, as the Close's panel says it

Maya’s session is restored for her before she has touched anything. A companion finishes her search and suggests she wants to stop. Her record keeps her old name “for continuity of care”. Her photographs come back improved. A headset offers an immersive session. Then a friend’s invitation, inside that same headset, leads her somewhere the system cannot classify — a room where being trans is fine — and the update fails. This room is inferred. Around it, the record is not: in May 2026 the European Commission answered more than a million people asking for a ban on conversion practices with a non-binding recommendation, and declined to propose a law; organised campaigns against trans people’s care run across Europe. The room extends patterns that are already documented — autocomplete, retained records, photo enhancement, “support” agents — into a system that presents itself as care. What it cannot classify is other people.

It finished your search for you and opened a support agent.

documented confidence: high for the pattern, low for any single detail

Rewire News Group's investigation (15 July 2026) found it discouraged gender-affirming care in every scenario its reporter tested. 2026 — the ball

The closing scene draws structural and visual influence from ballroom culture, and invents everything it shows: the houses, the categories, the names and the announcements are ours, and no real ball, house or person is depicted. The category names are quoted from this piece's own apparatus rather than from ballroom's. It does borrow the form of a ball (a category called, a house, the walk), because that form is what the scene honours, and it credits where the form comes from.

This is an outsider's homage made in a Norwegian university project. The culture it is indebted to is living and currently led by its own practitioners, and the reading below is where to start rather than where to stop.

LGB Alliance argued that affirming a gender-nonconforming or gender-dysphoric child as trans is itself a form of conversion therapy, because the child would otherwise grow up lesbian, gay or bisexual. It said so in a letter to all MPs on 30 March 2021, and again, in stronger words, in an email to clergy on 2 February 2022 under the subject "Transing away the gay" (reported by VICE, which saw it). Both asked that gender identity be kept out of the proposed ban on conversion therapy. No credible evidence shows that affirming a child turns someone who would otherwise be lesbian, gay or bisexual into a trans person. The Global Project Against Hate and Extremism lists LGB Alliance's Australian and Irish affiliates as anti-transgender in its reports on far-right hate groups; they reject the label. This is speech by a named organisation with a documented aim, and the piece treats it as such. It is not the gender-exploratory clinical debate, on which the piece holds no position and renders both readings unresolved.

2026 — the ball

documented confidence: high

Ball culture is a Black and Latino LGBTQ+ subculture, built principally by Black and Latina trans women, gender-nonconforming people and gay men. Its modern house-and-ball form took shape in Harlem in the early 1970s. Crystal LaBeija's on-camera objection to racist judging at a 1967 pageant is preserved in the film The Queen (1968); the House of LaBeija, founded with Lottie LaBeija, dates its first ball to 1972.

  • Royal House of LaBeija, history
  • Marlon M. Bailey, "Structures of Kinship" (Anthropology Now, 2021), PDF
documented confidence: high

A house is a chosen family led by a mother and/or father who take in and mentor children, and the kinship is not metaphorical: housing, food, money, health information and care have passed through houses, as much as any one house could give. Houses that followed LaBeija include the House of Xtravaganza (1982, founded by Hector Valle, with Angie Xtravaganza as its formative house mother) and the House of Ninja (1982, founded by Willi Ninja).

  • Marlon M. Bailey, Butch Queens Up in Pumps (University of Michigan Press, 2013)
  • "Constructing Home and Family", Journal of Gay & Lesbian Social Services (2008)
  • Los Angeles ballroom community study
  • Smithsonian NMAAHC, Hector Xtravaganza
  • Google Arts & Culture, Willi Ninja
documented confidence: high

Ballroom's "realness" categories are not inventions unrelated to the outside world — they are skilled re-performances of the categories the dominant culture already enforces, often at real cost, against the same people every day. Marlon M. Bailey's ethnography, and José Esteban Muñoz's concept of disidentification (1999) as scholars have applied it to ballroom, describe this as working on and against those categories from inside them. What was taken back is who declares, who performs and who judges.

  • Marlon M. Bailey, "Gender/Racial Realness", Feminist Studies (2011)
  • Marlon M. Bailey, Butch Queens Up in Pumps (University of Michigan Press, 2013)
  • University of Minnesota Press, Disidentifications (1999)
  • Disidentification applied to ballroom (2024)
disputed confidence: medium

The most-cited documentary about this culture, Paris Is Burning (1990), was made by Jennie Livingston, a white filmmaker from outside it, and bell hooks's 1992 essay "Is Paris Burning?" is its best-known critique: that the film centres its director's gaze, leaves her own position unexamined, and does not account for the power between her and the people she filmed. Ballroom's dance, language and aesthetics have repeatedly been taken into mainstream culture, with recurring fights over credit, pay and who benefits; ballroom's own members disagree about particular cases.

The words

The words these networks used in this year, and what they mean. A word marked “not supported” is a claim that health bodies and research do not support.

2026 — for trans people: confusion, trauma, peer influence, ‘gender ideology’, in the softer language of many factors.

same-sex attraction What it claims: not supported
A phrase ministries prefer to “gay” or “lesbian”. It frames attraction as a feeling to be reduced, not part of who someone is. Mainstream health bodies reject that framing.
unwanted same-sex attraction What it claims: disputed
“Unwanted” suggests a free choice. Research finds the wish to change usually comes from religious or family pressure, and many people exposed to change efforts were minors.
overcomer What it claims: not supported
A word that treats attraction as an enemy. The “overcome, don't eliminate” version is a retreat from earlier promises of change, not a finding that it works.
brokenness What it claims: not supported
A word that treats sexuality as damage. No orientation is a disorder; the damage described in research comes from stigma and from change efforts themselves.
gender confusion What it claims: not supported
“Gender confusion” is not a medical term. Current diagnostic manuals use “gender dysphoria” or “gender incongruence”. The word has been used to read ordinary gender nonconformity as a problem.
reintegrative therapy What it claims: disputed
A newer name for therapy linked to reparative therapy. Its providers deny it aims to change orientation; researchers and forensic experts classify it with change efforts.
SOGICE Documented
Short for efforts to change or suppress sexual orientation, gender identity or expression. Used by researchers and survivors; practitioners usually call it something gentler.
conversion practices Documented
An umbrella term for efforts to change or suppress who someone is. Major health bodies reject it. It is banned in a growing number of places and rebrands when banned.
accountability partner What it claims: disputed
A peer asked to monitor and report someone's behaviour. Support in itself is not harmful; here it was used to police a person's identity and treat lapses as failure.
freedom, healing, restoration What it claims: not supported
Words that present being gay as a captivity or injury. The research on these programmes finds the “freedom” was rarely delivered, and the harm that followed was real.
affirming What it claims: disputed
“Affirming” has two meanings. Therapists use it for acceptance without a set outcome. Change programmes used it for affirming “masculinity” or “femininity” as defined by them.
gender ideology A political frame, not a finding
A label used since the 2010s for gender studies, trans rights and sex education as one hidden agenda. Researchers describe it as a political frame, not a scientific claim.
social contagion What it claims: disputed
The claim that gender identity spreads among friends or online. It began as parents' reports; no validated clinical entity exists. Reviews call the topic contested and caution against simple explanations.
desistance What it claims: disputed
“Desistance” means a child's gender-variant feelings fading. Studies from the 1980s onward are disputed over how they counted children and what the numbers mean for care today.
detransition What it claims: disputed
Some people stop or reverse a gender transition, for many reasons, including regret, stigma, family pressure and changed understanding. Both their care and the way campaigns use their stories matter.
just exploring What it claims: disputed
“Exploratory” therapy says it only helps people think. Supporters deny it is conversion therapy; critics say it steers people away from transition. The question is disputed.
chosen family The community's own word
Family made by choice and care, not blood. For people rejected by their families, it can be a source of safety and support.
