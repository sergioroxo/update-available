2016 — Vera, at her desk

Nobody in this room is treated. The writing is edited.

The story

It is 2016. Vera works from her room. She corrects other women's writing for a platform called GracePlatform. They are lesbian women, writing about their own lives. She works one rule at a time: remove anything unresolved, add the scripture. Apply, or skip. Both choices are kept, and neither is marked right.

A friendly voice called Lambient asks her one question. Either answer works. The board offers more jobs as updates: answer families' calls, post to the group, tag a video. She may take them or leave them.

One afternoon the group chat on her phone is about a law. Malta has just made conversion practices an offence, by a unanimous vote of its parliament. It was the first country in Europe to do so. That is a real fact, and it is not ours. The newspaper that reports it in the piece is invented. The law is not.

Nobody in the room says anything. Content rules and moderation work were among the tools these networks used to reach people at scale. Then the platform announces that it is joining a larger service, and an update arrives.

GracePlatform, Lambient, the group and every person in this year are invented. Malta's Act LV of 2016 is real. The record is thin on the private messages of accountability groups in 2016. The group in the piece is written into that gap openly: an invented name, invented members, invented sentences, and a documented shape.

2016 — the platform and the group

documented confidence: high

Peer accountability groups are a real instrument of ex-gay ministry, not an invented device. In programmes such as Living Waters, membership is closed and justified by confidentiality, the group sits inside a structured course, and it is facilitated — coordinators, small-group leaders, assistant leaders and pastoral overseers are named roles, and leaders are trained. The group in the piece has a facilitator for this reason and is not a leaderless circle.

  • Desert Stream Ministries, Living Waters Leader's Guide (June 2024), PDF
  • Jowett et al., Conversion therapy: an evidence assessment and qualitative study (Government Equalities Office, 2021)
documented confidence: high

Accountability covers far more than sexual acts. Programme material such as Living Waters' asks about family history, trauma, and relational and sexual concerns, and its teaching names emotional dependency and codependency; accountability software, adjacent to it, reports what a person looked at online. A member disclosing a hard night on which nothing happened is the documented shape, not a softening of it.

  • Desert Stream Ministries, Living Waters Leader's Guide (June 2024), PDF
  • Robinson & Spivey, "Putting Lesbians in Their Place", Social Sciences (2015)
documented confidence: high

In the programme material, a reported lapse is framed as something to confess, pray through and keep working on — honesty, confession, forgiveness, healing prayer, boundaries. No rule was found that a lapse means removal (though breaking the group's confidentiality can). No source was found for a formal escalation ladder (warning, sponsor call, removal), and the piece therefore contains none.

  • Desert Stream Ministries, Living Waters Leader's Guide (June 2024), PDF

The women's strand of ex-gay ministry developed a vocabulary of its own, alongside the masculinised 'gender deficit' literature written for men: EMOTIONAL DEPENDENCY above all, alongside enmeshment, relational wounds, boundaries, unmet emotional need and a femininity to be recovered. Robinson and Spivey's peer-reviewed discourse analysis (Social Sciences 4(3), 2015) identifies 'emotional dependency' as a characteristic euphemism for female homosexuality. Every line of the era's course, group and podcast copy is written from this register.

Testimony became media. Ex-gay ministries turned personal stories of change into repeatable objects: Love in Action's own history begins with its founder's "testimony and a newspaper ad"; Focus on the Family's Love Won Out conferences (from 1998) put former-gay speakers on stage as evidence; the "Truth in Love" campaign ran such stories from 1998 as newspaper advertisements and, from 1999, as television commercials ending in a contact number; and the 2004 documentary I Do Exist gathered five stories with expert framing and was sold on VHS and DVD as a discussion starter for churches, schools and universities. By 2008 its producer had disowned the film as evidence of change and was offering it only on limited terms; one of its participants later wrote publicly about leaving the movement. Era 2's Your Story is that life cycle: his record, his words, the ministry's story, its media object, and a doorway for somebody else.

Play FloppySheep

FloppySheep is on Vera's phone, one tap away while her work waits. It is the platform's own mascot game: “a Lamby game”, its footer says. The piece says nothing about her playing it, and nothing she does in it is filed.

This is the same game, with its song. The Back arrow, or the phone's button, goes to the phone's home screen; tap the lamb there to play again.

How to play

Tap to hop, and tap again in the air to lift her a little higher. When she touches a lamb, it follows her. The programme's pressures cost you a lamb, never a life. The level lasts as long as the song. ♪ turns the song off.

On a keyboard, once you have pressed the phone: Space or Z is a tap.

Why it cannot be won

This game is the other way round. Nothing in it judges her: lambs drift away, and that is people choosing, never a loss. The platform's fireworks run out of fuel on their own at the end of the song. No score is kept after a run.

In the piece the game is optional. Nothing waits on it, and nothing is lost if it is never played.

The words

The words these networks used in this year, and what they mean. A word marked “not supported” is a claim that health bodies and research do not support.

2016 — for lesbian women: the mother wound, emotional dependency, abuse, a ‘spiritual fallacy’.

same-sex attraction What it claims: not supported
A phrase ministries prefer to “gay” or “lesbian”. It frames attraction as a feeling to be reduced, not part of who someone is. Mainstream health bodies reject that framing.
unwanted same-sex attraction What it claims: disputed
“Unwanted” suggests a free choice. Research finds the wish to change usually comes from religious or family pressure, and many people exposed to change efforts were minors.
the struggle A moral frame, not a finding
A word that casts being gay as a battle to be fought. The people who use it describe their own desires as a temptation; major health bodies do not.
struggler A moral frame, not a finding
What ministries call a gay person who resists their own desire. It makes the fight the person’s name, so the fight never ends. Members of these groups used it of themselves; the research records the word, it does not endorse it.
the lifestyle What it claims: not supported
A word that treats being gay as a habit or a choice. Attraction is not chosen, and health bodies describe it as a normal variation of human sexuality.
ex-gay What it claims: not supported
“Ex-gay” claims that orientation can be left behind. Research finds change efforts rarely change attraction and often harm. Many people who tried now call themselves ex-ex-gay.
overcomer What it claims: not supported
A word that treats attraction as an enemy. The “overcome, don't eliminate” version is a retreat from earlier promises of change, not a finding that it works.
brokenness What it claims: not supported
A word that treats sexuality as damage. No orientation is a disorder; the damage described in research comes from stigma and from change efforts themselves.
root causes What it claims: not supported
The idea that being gay is a symptom of a hidden hurt. Researchers reviewing the evidence found no such root; theories blaming family patterns have been discredited.
the wound What it claims: not supported
The claim that a distant father or smothering mother makes a child gay. Studies do not support it; a painful childhood is real for some, but does not decide orientation.
emotional dependency What it claims: not supported
A term for treating lesbian love as a disorder of “needing too much”. Researchers who studied the movement describe it as a euphemism, not a clinical finding.
gender confusion What it claims: not supported
“Gender confusion” is not a medical term. Current diagnostic manuals use “gender dysphoria” or “gender incongruence”. The word has been used to read ordinary gender nonconformity as a problem.
reparative therapy What it claims: not supported
A trademarked name for therapy aimed at reducing same-sex attraction. Mainstream associations oppose it; studies find it does not work and can harm.
reintegrative therapy What it claims: disputed
A newer name for therapy linked to reparative therapy. Its providers deny it aims to change orientation; researchers and forensic experts classify it with change efforts.
accountability partner What it claims: disputed
A peer asked to monitor and report someone's behaviour. Support in itself is not harmful; here it was used to police a person's identity and treat lapses as failure.
freedom, healing, restoration What it claims: not supported
Words that present being gay as a captivity or injury. The research on these programmes finds the “freedom” was rarely delivered, and the harm that followed was real.
affirming What it claims: disputed
“Affirming” has two meanings. Therapists use it for acceptance without a set outcome. Change programmes used it for affirming “masculinity” or “femininity” as defined by them.
gender ideology A political frame, not a finding
A label used since the 2010s for gender studies, trans rights and sex education as one hidden agenda. Researchers describe it as a political frame, not a scientific claim.
just exploring What it claims: disputed
“Exploratory” therapy says it only helps people think. Supporters deny it is conversion therapy; critics say it steers people away from transition. The question is disputed.
chosen family The community's own word
Family made by choice and care, not blood. For people rejected by their families, it can be a source of safety and support.
born that way Half right, and the half that matters is wrong
Scientists do not know exactly why orientation varies, and no single cause has been found. That does not show it can be changed; research finds attempts do not work. A church agency trained its counsellors (module captured 2005) to call ‘born that way’ a lie.
