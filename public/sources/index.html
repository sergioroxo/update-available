YOUR UPDATE HAS FAILED — sources

A browser and WebXR piece from SurvivingSOGICE (University of Bergen, Center for Digital Narrative) about how conversion-practice networks — efforts to change a person's sexual orientation or gender identity — have reached queer people online. The fiction is built from documented history, never from the people who lived it: every organisation and product in the piece is invented, and no survivor's words are used. This page lists what it is built from.

documented Documented — this happened, and the sources show it.

Daniel is sixteen. His mother has found a “family companion” programme on a message board and printed its questionnaire for him. It asks whether he has corrected the way he walks, talks and sits. 2003 AUDIT

documented Documented — this happened, and the sources show it.

Daniel is an adult, in the same room. The programme now lives on his computer as software. Every morning it asks how he is — Steady, Struggling, Grateful or Tired — and keeps the answer. It watches who he talks to; when a message arrives from someone outside, the programme sees it first. Daily self-reports, logging and the monitoring of contacts are documented features of accountability software of the period. Here they carry the demand to change without anyone saying it out loud.

Conversion-practice networks work across borders: annual conferences with delegates from dozens of countries, retreats held abroad, paid online courses. 2026 RESURGE

imagined Imagined — the piece's own projection from what is documented, and marked as such.

Maya’s session is restored for her before she has touched anything. A companion finishes her search and suggests she wants to stop. Her record keeps her old name “for continuity of care”. Her photographs come back improved. A headset offers an immersive session. Then a friend’s invitation, inside that same headset, leads her somewhere the system cannot classify — a room where being trans is fine — and the update fails. This room is speculative. It extends patterns that are already documented — autocomplete, retained records, photo enhancement, “support” agents — into a system that presents itself as care. What it cannot classify is other people.

What this room shows, and where to read it

The restored session documented

It finished your search for you and opened a support agent.

documented confidence: high for the pattern, low for any single detail

Detrans.ai, a chatbot that answers from retrieved public posts of a detransition support forum, launched in September 2025 — built, as reported, by one software engineer, and moved onto an open-weight model after the frontier models kept contradicting the answers it was built to give. Rewire News Group's investigation (15 July 2026) found it discouraged gender-affirming care in every scenario its reporter tested. No conversion-practice organisation was found building or commissioning it: an individual built the tool and an organisation promoted the link. The apparatus is an adopter, not a developer, and that is worse — it no longer needs a research budget, only a URL. The people whose words became that tool's voice were not asked either. The target is the apparatus that took them.

The care assignment documented

It took your details as the gate, and assigned care on your behalf.

disputed confidence: high

In 2022 a gay man who had been thrown out by his parents asked BetterHelp, the online therapy platform, for an LGBTQ-affirming therapist. He reported that it matched him with a therapist who told him "Either you sacrifice your family or you sacrifice being gay", answered "Good" when he said he had not been intimate with a man, and told him going home would be harder once he had (Wall Street Journal, 18 Dec 2022; Out, 22 Dec 2022). BetterHelp and the therapist declined to comment. The intake form was the gate; the match was the care. Era 4's step 1 (confirm your details before care begins) and step 3 (the care taken on your behalf) are that shape.

The termination imagined

It ended the session for your safety, and named the reason.

documented confidence: medium — sourced once, not yet its own pass

LGB Alliance argued that affirming a gender-nonconforming or gender-dysphoric child as trans is itself a form of conversion therapy, because the child would otherwise grow up lesbian, gay or bisexual. It said so in a letter to all MPs on 30 March 2021, and again, in stronger words, in an email to clergy on 2 February 2022 under the subject "Transing away the gay" (reported by VICE, which saw it). Both asked that gender identity be kept out of the proposed ban on conversion therapy. No credible evidence shows that affirming a child turns someone who would otherwise be lesbian, gay or bisexual into a trans person. The Global Project Against Hate and Extremism lists LGB Alliance's Australian and Irish affiliates as anti-transgender in its reports on far-right hate groups; they reject the label. This is speech by a named organisation with a documented aim, and the piece treats it as such. It is not the gender-exploratory clinical debate, on which the piece holds no position and renders both readings unresolved.

2026 — the ball

documented confidence: high

Ball culture is a Black and Latino LGBTQ+ subculture, built principally by Black and Latina trans women, gender-nonconforming people and gay men. Its modern house-and-ball form took shape in Harlem in the early 1970s. Crystal LaBeija's on-camera objection to racist judging at a 1967 pageant is preserved in the film The Queen (1968); the House of LaBeija, founded with Lottie LaBeija, dates its first ball to 1972.

  • Royal House of LaBeija, history
  • Marlon M. Bailey, "Structures of Kinship" (Anthropology Now, 2021), PDF
documented confidence: high

A house is a chosen family led by a mother and/or father who take in and mentor children, and the kinship is not metaphorical: housing, food, money, health information and care have passed through houses, as much as any one house could give. Houses that followed LaBeija include the House of Xtravaganza (1982, founded by Hector Valle, with Angie Xtravaganza as its formative house mother) and the House of Ninja (1982, founded by Willi Ninja).

Ballroom's "realness" categories are not inventions unrelated to the outside world — they are skilled re-performances of the categories the dominant culture already enforces, often at real cost, against the same people every day. Marlon M. Bailey's ethnography, and José Esteban Muñoz's concept of disidentification (1999) as scholars have applied it to ballroom, describe this as working on and against those categories from inside them. What was taken back is who declares, who performs and who judges.

The most-cited documentary about this culture, Paris Is Burning (1990), was made by Jennie Livingston, a white filmmaker from outside it, and bell hooks's 1992 essay "Is Paris Burning?" is its best-known critique: that the film centres its director's gaze, leaves her own position unexamined, and does not account for the power between her and the people she filmed. Ballroom's dance, language and aesthetics have repeatedly been taken into mainstream culture, with recurring fights over credit, pay and who benefits; ballroom's own members disagree about particular cases.

LGB Alliance argued that affirming a gender-nonconforming or gender-dysphoric child as trans is itself a form of conversion therapy, because the child would otherwise grow up lesbian, gay or bisexual.
