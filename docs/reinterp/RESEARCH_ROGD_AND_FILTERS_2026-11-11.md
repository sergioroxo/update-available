## A. The 2019 correction to Littman (2018)

OPENED: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6424391 ("Correction: Parent reports of adolescents and young adults perceived to show signs of a rapid onset of gender dysphoria", PLOS ONE 14(3): e0214157, 19 March 2019).
OPENED: the corrected article, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0202330 (first published 16 Aug 2018; the page links to the correction).

Verbatim phrases from the correction (all documentary):
- Sites: parents "may have viewed the recruitment information from one of at least four sites with varied perspectives."
- Stance: "three of the sites that posted recruitment information expressed cautious or negative views" about "medical and surgical interventions for gender dysphoric adolescents". One site "is perceived to be pro-gender-affirming".
- First contact: "Data about where participants first heard about this study were not collected."
- Diagnosis: ROGD "is not a formal mental health diagnosis at this time."
- Whose data: "This report did not collect data from the adolescents and young adults (AYAs) or clinicians".

- The study is framed as developing "hypotheses". The correction says the report does not validate ROGD as a phenomenon.

Note a wrinkle. The corrected paper itself names three recruitment sites for the first-week posting and says all were critical of rapid medical transition. A fourth site spread the notice by sharing. ## B. What ROGD's proponents claim, and what answers it

### B1. The claimed signs and causes
Source for the list: Littman 2018, OPENED (parent-report survey, n=256). Numbers are parent reports, from the paper's own text.
- Sudden announcement: 80.9% of parents said it came "out of the blue" (the paper's phrase, as the fetch showed it). Eligibility needed a parent to say onset was "sudden or rapid" and began during or after puberty. There was no clinical definition. (documentary: it is what the paper reports)
- Friend groups / "clusters": 36.8% said most of the friend group became trans-identified. (documentary as a report; contested as proof of cause)
- Social media / internet: 63.5% said internet or social media use rose before the announcement. (same)
- Popularity: 60.7% said the young person gained popularity after coming out, where known.
- Prior mental health and neurodevelopmental conditions: 62.5%. Prior trauma: 48.4%. Self-harm before onset: 45.0%. The paper also lists social anxiety and peer difficulties. I did not get an autism figure from the part of the text I read. [VERIFY SOURCE] for autism.
- Parents' own view: 76.5% felt their child was wrong about being trans. At the time of the survey 83.2% were still trans-identified and 5.5% had desisted (paper's figures).
- "Affirming" clinicians and schools: these are common in advocates' versions. I did not find them as measured items in the part of the paper I read (the second half of the page was not read). Treat as [VERIFY SOURCE] for Littman; they are documented as claims in advocacy and in the HHS framing (B2).

### B2. Documents and people that use the frame (characterise only)

## D. Two paragraphs for the artist's research article

**1. A diagnosis that is not real, and the passive voice.**
"Rapid-onset gender dysphoria" sounds like a medical term. It is not one. The 2019 correction to the paper that coined it says it "is not a formal mental health diagnosis" and that the study "did not collect data from" the young people or their clinicians. The parents who answered came from at least four sites, three of which expressed "cautious or negative views" of medical interventions, and the correction says it was not recorded where each parent first heard of the study. The idea still travels as if it were a finding, in bills, reports and books, and professional bodies such as CAAPS say it should be dropped from clinical use. The grammar of the idea matters as much as the facts. "It spread." "She was influenced." "It happened to her." Nobody in those sentences is the one who knows her. In "Second Thoughts" the AI does the acting for Maya: it mutes her friend and runs her computer. The piece mirrors the frame by taking the verbs away from her. The harm is shown by who is left as the subject of the sentence. (Contested overall; the Cass Review calls peer influence "potentially the most contested explanation" [VERIFY SOURCE: paras 8.24-8.25], and the piece leaves both readings standing.)

**2. Chatbots as the newest carrier.**
The frame used to travel in a book, a parent forum or a government report. A chatbot can carry it into a private chat at night with a person who is already afraid. One public example is a chatbot that calls itself "the collective consciousness of detransitioners" and answers from r/detrans posts and YouTube videos; its own page warns that quoted material may include "bot-generated or misleading content". I found no source that it names or applies ROGD, so the piece should not say it does. What is documented is the shape: a tool that speaks in the first person for a group, with no clinician and no way to check who is behind each line. The invented "Second Thoughts" borrows that shape and fills it with the contagion frame. That is the piece's own invention, and the article should say so. Mainstream chatbots draw complaints from the other side too, so the point is not one product but the form: confident, private and unsupervised. (Opened: detrans.ai home page, 11 Nov 2026. Second-hand: the news about Snapchat's My AI and the developer claims.)
