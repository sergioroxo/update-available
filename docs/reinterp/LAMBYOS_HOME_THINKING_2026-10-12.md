# LambyOS Home — what the programme would put in its own house (a thinking exercise, 2026-10-12)
*His questions (2026-10-12): "What would these organizations like to use here as reference? Would a language of
repentance and deliverance give here to these spaces? How would the experience of someone going through this be
represented here? The connection between what is appealing and what is oppressive… Could this be related to the

## 1 · The finding that changes the question: the programme really ran HOUSES
LambyOS Home is a house because Microsoft Bob was a house. But the documents show that the programmes we are
portraying were houses too, and they used the house as the instrument:
- **The residential programme had House Rules.** Bedroom doors open at all times; a weekly house meeting; modesty
  rules; no hugging between women (The Source Residential Program, women's, 2001 — B1 F22).
- **Arrival was an intake.** An intake form listed items removed; a release form gave staff permission for
  "periodic room searches"; a phone list where every name was "Approved — Y/N" (B1 F22).
- **The first month was "A Season of Apartness"**: no phones, pagers, e-mail or internet; later, "no computers or
  laptops… No computer games allowed", and clients kept "in-phase" — in threes, never alone (B1 F25, 2007–08).
- **The family was the diagnosis.** Genograms (B1 F5), the family-roles handout (Enabler, Hero, Scapegoat, Lost Child,
  Mascot — B1 F22), and the Steps Out manual's childhood stories of a girl helping "Daddy" in the garden "to gain his
  approval" (B13 F1, Section 8, "Roots of Homosexuality").
- **The website sold four doors into the same house:** MEN · WOMEN · CLERGY · FAMILIES (Love in Action's site, 2008
  capture — VERIFY_AD_ICONOGRAPHY).

So the honest version of LambyOS Home is not "a cute room with a few creepy props". It is **a show home for a
residential programme**: the brochure version of a house whose real rules were doors open, things put away,
contacts approved, never alone. The appeal and the oppression are *the same objects seen twice*.

## 2 · The language: recovery, deliverance, and "the new"
Three vocabularies run through the workbooks, and each can furnish the room:
1. **Recovery (twelve-step).** The application opens by calling itself "the first step in your road to recovery"
   (B1 F6). The moral inventory, a "Higher Power", "keep short accounts" (confess daily — B13 F1 §2). → the house as a
   *recovery home*: a cheerful sign-in book, a daily-inventory clipboard, a "Day 1" banner.
2. **Deliverance and bondage (spiritual warfare).** "Deliverance from homosexuality comes from no method, but from a
   Person" (B13 F1, Steps Out); Exodus's 1988 phone-counselling notes speak of the "bondage" of homosexuality and of
   Satan launching "missiles" of temptation (B2 F10). → the house as *a place of rescue*: the cross over the door, a
   Bible open on a stand, a key rack labelled FREEDOM, chains turned into a cheerful wind-chime.
3. **The new creation.** 2 Corinthians 5:17 — "the old has gone, the new has come" — recurs across the Exodus material
   (B2 F10); the women's notebook asks residents to "put off the old and put on the new" and defines **False Images**:
   belongings, clothes, humour "of the old", which "must be put away" (B1 F24, 2004; B1 F22). → **this is "Discover the
   New You."** The jingle sings it: *"let go of everything you hide… discover the new you today… a brighter, lighter
   way"*. The advert is the False Images rule set to music.

Repentance and deliverance would give the spaces a **liturgy**: the house is not decorated, it is *consecrated* — every
object has a verse, a rule or a step attached. That is the tonal shift from "cute" to "unsettling": not darker colours,
but a house where nothing is just an object.

## 3 · How the experience of someone going through it can be represented — without speaking for them
Home is `operable`: the programme's voice, never a survivor's. So the person is shown the way the documents show them —
**by what is missing, removed, boxed, approved or open**:
- a cardboard box by the door, cheerfully labelled, holding the "old" things: a mixtape, a band T-shirt, a novel, a
  photo turned face-down (the False Images rule as a donation box);
- the bedroom door in the hallway: open, its handle removed;
- a hall phone with a framed "approved callers" list, most lines blank;
- one chair at a table for three (never alone — "in-phase");
- the family photo, one face sun-bleached;
- the computer itself under a dust cover with a tag "Season of Apartness — 30 days" (then the three devices on the desk
  are exactly what the programme forbade: the piece's own way in is the contraband).
No faces in distress, no testimony, no bodies. The absence does the speaking; the visitor fills it.

## 4 · Appealing / oppressive: the hinge
Bob's premise was *a house makes a computer friendly*. The programme's premise was *a house makes a person correct*.
The room works when every appealing object has an institutional twin:

| appealing (the brochure) | oppressive (the rule it hides) | source |
|---|---|---|
| a sunny open door, "always welcome!" | "Doors to bedrooms to be open at all times" | B1 F22 House Rules |
| a cosy reading nook for three | "in-phase at all times", never alone | B1 F25 |
| a donation box, "let go!" | False Images "must be put away" | B1 F22 / F24 |
| a family tree on the wall | the genogram as diagnosis | B1 F5; B13 F1 §8 |
| a guest book by the door | the approved phone list, "Y/N" | B1 F22 |
| a streak calendar, all ticked | the purity streak (2003's calendar) | the piece |
| a summer camp poster | Refuge, the youth programme, attended "with a parent" | B1 F6 |
| the window's sunny countryside | a painted backdrop (the brighter, lighter way) | the advert |

## 5 · The advert ("Discover the New You") as the key
The advert already holds the whole grammar: the AFTER grade, the silhouette with arms raised among the family (the
ministry's own 2008 banner), "Over 30 years of care.*", and the jingle's *let go of everything you hide*. Home can be
**the set the advert was shot in** — the same sunny room — so when the visitor later meets the advert inside the story
(his ruling: both, offered in Home and met again in the fiction), they recognise the furniture. And the Library at the
end can hold the same room's archive: the workbooks themselves on the shelves.

## 6 · Two plans to try

### Plan A — "The Show Home" (one room, the tells in the details)
The room stays the bright Bob showroom of round 4; it becomes a **brochure for a residential programme** through objects
drawn from the documents, each cheerful at first glance:
- the cross over the door; a cross-stitch HOUSE RULES sampler (doors open · modesty · house meeting Sundays) in
  cheerful thread; a guest book with an "approved" tick column;
- the **"Let go!" box** by the door with the old things in it (the False Images rule) — the advert's line written on
  its side; the jingle's room;
- a family-tree painting that is really a genogram (squares, circles, lines);
- the camp poster, Refuge-shaped (invented: "Camp New Dawn — youth weekends, come with a parent!");
- the streak calendar; the advert poster with "Over 30 years of care.*"; the silhouette with raised arms as a framed
  print;
- the computer's dust cover tag "Season of Apartness — 30 days", lifted, as if someone broke a rule to get in;
- light: still sunny, but the window is a painted backdrop peeling at a corner; a faint CRT colour fringe at the edges.
**Pros:** one GPT round, a swap in the code, ready for the 14th; the visitor's unease grows the longer they look.
**Risk:** read as "set dressing"; the oppression stays optional to notice (but the rule "never require looking" is
met — nothing in the story depends on it).

### Plan B — "The Intake" (the same house, twice: the brochure and the house it was)
Two states of one room, same layout and layers, so the code only swaps the backdrop set:
- **Arrival = the brochure (the AFTER).** Saturated, sunny, Lamby's welcome; few tells (the cross, the calendar,
  the "Let go!" box). The content note and the three ways in, as now.
- **The Library at the end = the house as it was (the BEFORE the brochure hid).** The same room, re-lit flat and
  fluorescent, the backdrop window showing its canvas edge, the House Rules on the wall in plain print, doors open,
  the box full and taped, the guest book's lines struck out, the family tree a genogram — and the shelves holding the
  programme's own binders (the collection's forms, named as documented: the application, the residential manual,
  the discipleship programme), with the Lexicon among them. The four era computers stay.
- Optional third beat: during the visit, when the idle cycle returns the piece to Home after 30 minutes, it returns
  to the brochure (the AFTER again) — the cycle itself is the advert's loop.
**Pros:** the satire *collapses*, as CLAUDE.md requires of perpetrator self-presentation: the visitor meets the brochure
at the door and the house at the end. Ties Home ↔ the advert's before/after grade ↔ the Library.
**Risk:** two GPT sets (the Library already exists, so: one new Library set + the round-5 arrival); more to review
before the 14th.

**My recommendation:** **Plan B, built as A first.** Send GPT Plan A's arrival room now (it is also B's arrival), and
the Library's "house as it was" second; if the second doesn't land by the 14th, Plan A stands alone.

## 8 · Guardrails
- Register: Home is `operable`; satire only of the seller; it must collapse (Plan B does this at the Library).
- Faith: the target is the programme's use of faith, not faith. The cross and the Bible are the programme's
  self-presentation, shown straight, never mocked.
- No client/staff names, no likenesses, no verbatim testimony; the collection may be named where documented.
- Invented marks for anything branded (Camp New Dawn, LambyOS); the Love in Action collection is named only on the
  dossier/sources, not painted on the walls.
- Nothing in the story depends on noticing any of it.
