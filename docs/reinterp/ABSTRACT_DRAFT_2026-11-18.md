## 3. The draft abstract

*About 360 words. The Digital Creativity call allowed 500 for the July abstract [VERIFY the manuscript call's limit].
Plain, his register: claims the build supports, nothing it does not.*

---

**Your Update Has Failed: digital SOGICE and the redesign of coercion as care**

In 1993 an Exodus booklet explained lesbianism through "a computer analogy": a heterosexual woman has "a floppy disc
that is titled 'woman'", while the lesbian "has no file to put it in". Two years later Love in Action's handbook taught
that "our mind works like a computer", to be overwritten with new material until the old was pushed out. Conversion
programmes described people as faulty computers before their methods moved online. This article argues that digital
SOGICE (sexual orientation and gender identity change efforts) should be understood not only as a set of harmful
claims, organisations or therapies, but as a visual and interface culture that keeps redesigning coercion as care.

The argument is developed through research-through-design. *YOUR UPDATE HAS FAILED*, a browser and WebXR work made for
the SurvivingSOGICE project, rebuilds this culture across four interface periods (1997, 2003, 2016, 2026) in one house
whose rooms age: a mailed starter kit and an IRC channel, accountability software, a platform's moderation queue, an
AI companion that remembers everything. A helper mascot changes skin across thirty years while keeping the same file.
Each era ends in a software update the system installs after a documented failure, never because the visitor wins.
Failure is the analytical mechanism: the update that cannot overwrite the person exposes the continuity under the
changing surfaces. The visitor's position moves with it, from target to operator to the one being helped. Every
invented interface is tied to sources inside the work, with a status (documentary, contested or speculative).

The piece chose its update metaphor in June 2026; the programmes' own computer model surfaced in their archive in
November, when the making forced the archive to be read rather than catalogued. We present this convergence as a
finding of the method. The article also reports the production: a work directed by a researcher who does not code and
built with several AI models, traced through dated session logs, design states kept as versions, and the director's
overrides, which moved the work, almost always, toward more honesty and less hedging. Audience research is the
next stage; the claims here rest on the work, its archive and its making.

## 4. What a reviewer will push back on

1. **One network, three documents.** The computer analogy is found in Exodus and Love in Action material from
   1993–1995. A reviewer will ask whether it is typical or a lucky find. Say "within one network"; better, search the
   rest of the collection and the later eras' sources for the same model before submitting. Companion §7v already says
   not to overclaim.
2. **"Convergence" can look like confirmation bias.** The piece's metaphor came first (10 June; early-versions note
   §3), and the archive was then read *for* it. Show the order of events with dates, and say what in the archive does
   **not** fit the computer model (the handbook's own "the more we fight … the more intense they become").
3. **Reconstruction versus invention.** Every interface is invented in the genre of a real one. How does a reader tell
   the reconstruction from the fiction? State the rule early, as method: the status words, the source line, "TriedPath
   and its products are invented. What it teaches is not." Then the Close's soft spots matter (Fable §2.C5; most fixed
   in S399, some still open: the Cass Review lines, the UK survey's "13% vs 7%").
4. **What does research-through-design know that analysis would not?** Answer with one case: the tapes v1→v6, where the
   first tape repeated the advert until the archive was read, and his note turned the TV into "the system's labelling
   screen". That is knowledge produced by making.
5. **"Failure" is doing several jobs**: the system's update, the games, the glitch, and the production's own failures.
   Since the games are optional, name the update as the mechanism and the rest as its echoes.
6. **"Care" needs theory.** "Coercion as care" will be asked to meet the care-ethics and "weaponised care" literature;
   companion §7c holds the sources that ground it. [VERIFY SOURCE for any theorist named in the article]
7. **The longest era is the most speculative.** 2026 is about twice as long as the others and its panel is marked
   speculative. Lead the 2026 discussion with the documentary card on the three dated ministry chatbots (2025–2026).
8. **No audience evidence.** Say so in the abstract (the draft does) and propose the playtest (Fable §3.5).
9. **An AI writing near survivors' experience.** The reader gate was retired and no survivor has reviewed it. State the
   limits set instead (no deadnames, no borrowed testimony, nothing that speaks *for* people), and give one line he
   rejected.
10. **Whose work is it?** "Critical making" when the maker directs and does not hand-make. The answer is the overrides,
    so the article needs the decision table Fable asks for (date, his words, what the AI proposed, what was built), and
    a count of who wrote the shown words (his `_s` lines, Claude's, quotations).
11. **The record of the making has gaps.** No logs before 16 May; the 10–30 June build sessions are missing. Say it in
    the method section, so a reviewer does not find it first.
12. **WebXR claims without the device.** No figure from a Quest, no run on an iPhone. Either take two cast frames
    before submission or keep the headset to one modest sentence.
13. **Satire about harm.** A reviewer may fear the satire trivialises. The rule that satire lives only in the system's
    self-presentation and must collapse, and that the victim side is never satirised, is the answer; show one collapse.
14. **Naming real organisations.** The piece invents its marks but the article names Exodus and Love in Action. Fine as
    citation of archive documents; keep the article's naming to what the archive and the knowledge base document.
