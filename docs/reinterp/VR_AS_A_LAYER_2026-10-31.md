# VR as its own layer — a design proposal (31 Oct 2026)

*Written after his fifth headset pass and his ask: "there is a lot of creativity that I feel like you need to bring
here". Until now VR has been treated as the browser build made to survive in a headset. This proposes treating it as a
**layer of the work with its own meaning**: what the body does, what the hands touch, what the headset itself says.
Nothing here is built unless marked BUILT. Every proposal has an id (VR-nn) and says whether it needs his call.*

*Fixed ground (his rulings, VR_NOTES_2026-10-24): the piece never moves or turns the view by itself in a headset; a
change of place is the pixel dissolve; hands and controllers always both work; seated; a native Quest app is phase 2.
The ethics and register laws (felt / respite / operable) apply unchanged.*

---

## 1. The idea in one paragraph

In the browser you **watch** the system work on someone's room. On a phone you **hold** the room and turn it. In a
headset you are **sat in the chair**. The headset is the first mode where the piece can address the body directly,
and it is also — this is the turn — the newest interface in the history the piece tells. The four eras run from a
beige monitor to an AI voice in a visor; the visitor in a Quest is already wearing the fifth. So the VR layer should
do two things at once: make each era's interface **bodily** (you use its hardware with your hands), and let the last
era **fold back onto the headset you are wearing** ("VR in VR").

## 2. Each era's hardware, held — the interaction layer

The browser build has one input for thirty years: a click on a screen. VR can give each era its own body.

| era | the hardware | what the hands do in VR | proposal |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1997 | beige mouse, keyboard, floppy, CRT power button | rest your hand on the **mouse**: moving it on the desk moves the cursor; pinch = click. The keys clack when touched (no typing — the law stays: no free text) | **VR-01 the desk mouse** — needs his call (it is a new way to press, and slower than pointing) |
| 2003 | optical mouse, flip phone, the media player's tape | the same mouse, lighter; the **flip phone opens** when you pick it up | VR-02 the flip phone opens in the hand |
| 2016 | Vera's smartphone | the phone is the surface: pick it up, **thumb** the screen (poke), it buzzes in the hand when the group writes | VR-03 the phone in the hand is the era's main input — the monitor is secondary |
| 2026 | Maya's laptop and **the visor** | the visor is worn: bring it to your face to enter the programme (see §4) | VR-08 |

**Why it matters for the piece:** the intimacy of each period's technology is *how close it sits to the body* — a
desk, then a pocket, then a hand, then the face. VR is the only mode that can make the visitor feel that distance
shrink. **For the article** this is the clearest thing VR adds: the history of SOGICE's interfaces is a history of
them moving onto the body.

**VR-04 desk calibration (needs his call; strongly recommended).** In the setup, Lamby asks: *"Put your hand flat on a
table, if there is one."* The fingertip height becomes the desk height — the virtual desk meets the real one. The
visitor's elbows can rest where he wanted to rest them ("I want to put my elbows on the table"). Without a table, the
current seat stands. This is one press, it fixes most "too far / too high" reports for good, and it gives the haptic
grounding a seated piece needs.

## 3. Lamby in VR — the guide becomes a creature, then a voice, then nothing

The article's own line: *Lamby's transformation from a Clippy-like mascot into an ambient, disembodied voice.* In a
browser that transformation is drawn. In a headset it can be **felt in space**:

- **1997 — Lamby steps out of the monitor.** The balloon still appears on the screen, but when Lamby offers to take
  you to the thing to press ("Show me"), a small pixel Lamby (a flat sprite, always facing you, era palette) hops out
  of the CRT onto the desk and walks to the object — the floppy, the racket — and stands beside it, bouncing. Pressing
  Lamby = Yes. (**VR-05**, needs his call: it is the guide's first body.)
- **2003 — Lamby lives on the taskbar and on the desk** as the plush toy on the shelf that turns its head to you when
  it speaks. The balloon is on the monitor; the voice of the hint comes from the toy. (VR-05b)
- **2016 — Lamby is a notification.** No body: a badge that floats beside the phone and pulses; it follows the phone
  if you pick it up. (VR-05c)
- **2026 — Lamby is L, and L is everywhere.** Spatial audio from the visor first, then from *around* the visitor once
  the visor is on; no body at all. A soft light moves through the room when L speaks. (VR-05d)

The laws do not move: at most five unsolicited balloons per era, never in felt or respite scenes, always ignorable,
dismissal logged, no sound in Lamby's own voice (the 2026 voice is L's, which is the programme's, not the guide's).
"Take me there" in VR never moves the visitor: Lamby goes to the thing; the visitor turns their own head.

## 4. VR in VR — the visor in 2026 (**VR-08**, the strongest idea here; needs his call)

Maya's visor sits on the desk in 2026. In the browser, putting it on is a press and a cut. In a headset:

1. **You pick it up** (grab, as the Game Boy). It is light; a faint hum.
2. **You bring it to your face.** As it reaches the eyes, the edges of the view close into the visor's lens shape — a
   second frame inside the first. Inside: the programme's world (the Commons, the Restoration tool), at a slightly
   lower resolution and a slightly different colour, so the visitor feels the layer.
3. **The programme reads you.** Inside the visor the programme draws *your own hands* in its style — the fingertip
   cubes change to its palette, a soft outline "corrects" their shape. It is the system's oldest move — remaking the
   person as the product — done to the visitor's own body representation, not Maya's. (Operable register; never during
   a felt scene; no comment on it.)
4. **You take it off** by lifting it away — the room is still there, as it was, the lamp on. The respite is the room.

This is where the article's argument ("coercion redesigned as care") meets the medium: the visitor is wearing the
interface the piece is criticising, and the piece says so without a word.

**VR-09 the receipt counts the head (needs his call; ethics).** The Close's receipt already counts the hands that
touched Daniel/Vera/Maya's file. In VR it could add one line: *"Your head turned toward the bed 14 times. The programme
keeps this."* Head pose is the data every headset collects. Memory only, wiped with the ledger, never sent — the law
holds. It names the medium as a surveillance instrument. ⚑ His ethics call; it must not feel like a trap.

## 5. The flights, re-thought for a seated body

The browser flies the camera between rooms (the relocation, the descent). VR cannot (nausea, his ruling). Today VR
cuts under the pixel dissolve — correct, but it loses what the flight *meant*: the three rooms are one house across
thirty years.

**VR-06 the dollhouse (needs his call).** At an era change the room pixel-dissolves into a **model of the house held in
front of you**, about 60 cm wide: the three rooms, lit, from above. The new era's room glows. One beat (3–4 s), then
the model dissolves outward and you are seated in the new room. No camera movement at all — the world comes to the
visitor, the visitor never moves. The Close's overview (stars, then the house) can use the same model.

**VR-07 the update wraps around you.** The software update (notification → EULA → install → restart) is the era's
signature beat. In VR the install's progress is a ring of pixel blocks around the seat, filling; the changelog lines
appear on it as the ring turns (the ring turns, not the visitor). The glitch at the end shakes the ring, not the view.

## 6. What VR asks of the visitor — the honest list

- **Sit.** In a chair, ideally at a table. About 30–45 minutes. Stand up and the piece re-seats you after a second.
- **Turn.** The record is behind you in every era (the witness side). VR is where the turn is natural; the piece never
  turns you.
- **Reach.** Everything pressable within about 60 cm of the seat, or pressable by pointing from the seat.
- **Hands or controllers.** Pinch to press at a distance, touch to press near; the fingertip shows when it touches.
- **Look at small text.** The weakest point of the medium: pixel text needs to be closer or bigger than in the
  browser. Rule for VR: no running text below 14 px of the 512-px canvas on a surface farther than 70 cm.
- **Trust the dissolve.** A change of place is always a dissolve to colour pixels and back. It must never be a cut.

## 7. Interaction across the three modes — for the article

| | browser (drag-to-look) | phone / tablet (gyro-to-look) | headset (WebXR) |
|---|---|---|---|
| the body | hand on a mouse, eyes on a frame | the device held up, the body turns | seated; the head is the camera |
| looking | drag; the piece may conduct the camera | turn the device; drag composes with it | the head only; the piece never turns the view |
| pressing | a click on the room or the desktop | a tap (resolved on release) | a pinch at a distance, a touch up close; controller trigger |
| changing place | a flight (conducted, eased) | the same flight | a pixel dissolve; (proposed) the dollhouse |
| the guide | Lamby's balloon on the screen | the same | (proposed) Lamby with a body in 1997, a voice in 2026 |
| the record behind you | the flip (a turn performed for you) | a turn you make with your body | a turn you make with your body, the most natural of the three |
| what the medium says | a desktop simulator | a surveillance object in the hand | the newest interface in the history it tells |

## 9. What should be built first (my recommendation)

1. **VR-04 desk calibration** — fixes the body before anything else; one press.
2. **VR-05 Lamby steps out (1997)** — the guide he asked for, in the era where it matters most.
