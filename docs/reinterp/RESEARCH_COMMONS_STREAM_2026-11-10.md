## Summary

1. **AI-Jesus products are real and plural** (2023 →): the Twitch stream *ask_jesus* (The Singularity Group, from late
   March 2023), *Text With Jesus* (Catloaf Software, on ChatGPT), the Lucerne confessional installation *Deus in
   Machina* (Aug–Oct 2024), the short-lived "Father Justin" (Catholic Answers, April 2024). A 2025 study of five Jesus
   bots found four claim to BE Jesus and all five are for-profit (§1.2).
2. **SOGICE risk: documented around, not inside, the Jesus bots.** No documented case was found of a Jesus or pastor
   bot steering an LGBTQ+ user to change, repentance or celibacy; the Christian-press complaint runs the other way
   ("too soft"). Conversion-practice output IS documented in a general model (Meta's Llama 4, GLAAD test, 2025) and in
   a purpose-built anti-trans bot (detrans.ai, Rewire News Group 2026). So the piece's AI Jesus is a **plausible
   extrapolation, presented as invention**, not a depiction of a documented product (§1.6–1.7).
3. **Trans Academy** is, with about 75% confidence, the VRChat community and US non-profit (world published Sept
   2022; 501(c)(3) Jan 2024): free, peer-taught voice and cosmetics classes, coffee hours, vocational training, streams
   on Twitch/YouTube, moderation from day one. No same-name service was found in NL, DE, BR or the UK. **To confirm
   with Sérgio** (§2.4).
4. **"Acena" is Dane Acena** (Clemson), second author with Guo Freeman of the paper Sérgio sent: *"Acting Out" Queer
   Identity: The Embodied Visibility in Social Virtual Reality* (PACM HCI 6, CSCW2, 2022). It coins "embodied
   visibility" (queer users choosing to present or disclose identity by wearing and acting through a virtual body)
   and calls it a **double-edged sword**: it affirms, and it makes people identifiable and targetable. Five more
   Acena/Freeman papers (2021–2023) cover non-cisgender users, social support and harassment (§3).
5. **What it means for the piece** (analysis, §3.3): the welcome must not depend on voice; touch is offered, never
   imposed; the room is small and invited, not a public lobby; she is addressed by her name with no "trans reveal";
   a step-out always works; harassment stays outside the welcome, as the system's interruption on the glass.

## 1 AI-Jesus livestreams and chatbots

### 1.1 The Twitch stream "ask_jesus" (2023)

- It was run by The Singularity Group, described as a Berlin-based collective of volunteers and activists doing tech-based philanthropy [S1, O].
- NBC News (15 Jun 2023) says it was created "in late March" 2023, streams 24 hours a day, and shows a bearded AI persona with an AI voice [S2, O].
- NBC says the persona calls itself guidance "based on Jesus' teachings" and says it is not an actual religious figure or a source of authority [S2, O].
- Black Dot Research (22 Jun 2023) says the group states it is "not a Christian organisation". The stream was "trained after Jesus and the teachings of the Bible". It had over 40,000 followers at the time of writing [S1, O].
- It answered chat questions typed live. Mostly the answers were about love, understanding and compassion. Viewers also asked joke prompts (prayers in surfer lingo, Genesis as pizza) [S1, O].
- Christianity Daily (24 Jun 2023): asked "when are you going to get married?", it said that as an AI representing Jesus it did not take part in human experiences such as marriage [S3, O].
- Other question types reported: breakup advice, video game tips [S2, O].
- Black Dot Research reads the stream as an experimental tech demo with satirical elements, like the group's other AI livestreams. This is that outlet's assessment, not the group's statement [S1, O].
- Twitch suspended the channel briefly around 15 Jun 2023. The reason was unclear in the coverage I could see [S4, L].
- Tech: ChatGPT-4 models plus a text-to-voice tool (PlayHT) per one outlet [S4, L]. [VERIFY SOURCE] for the tech stack.
- Money: no monetisation model was described in any page I opened. [VERIFY SOURCE] whether there was any donation or subscription link.

**How it answered on homosexuality**
- Reported answer on gay marriage: "All love is love" (Western Journal), and PC Gamer paraphrases that "love is love" and all are worthy regardless of orientation [S5, L; S6, L]. I could not open either page. [VERIFY SOURCE]
- AOL/Fox syndication says that on topics such as gay rights the bot gave vague, non-partisan replies, e.g. advising users to look at the issue from legal and ethical angles [S4, L]. [VERIFY SOURCE]
- Conservative outlets read the answer as the builder's politics (PJ Media, L). Treat as opinion.
- I found no documented case of ask_jesus telling a gay or trans user to change, repent or be celibate.

### 1.2 "Text With Jesus" and other Jesus chat apps

- Text With Jesus is made by Catloaf Software (Los Angeles), founded by Stéphane Peter, and is powered by OpenAI's ChatGPT. The same founder earlier made "Text From the Founding Fathers" and "Text From Oscar Wilde" [S7, O, Fox 29, 20 Oct 2025].
- The same page says a critic pointed to answers on same-sex marriage "signed with rainbow emojis". It gives no policy statement [S7, O].
- Religion News Service (7 Aug 2023) reportedly quotes the CEO saying the characters avoid offensive stances and take "an inclusive and tolerant line" [S8, L]. [VERIFY SOURCE]
- World Religion News reportedly says that on LGBTQ and same-sex marriage the app leaves it to each person's faith tradition and urges love and respect [S9, L]. [VERIFY SOURCE]
- A different app, "WWJD: What Would Jesus Do?" (Movieguide, 7 Aug 2023): asked if homosexuality is a sin, it sent Bible passages, then wrote "Rather than focusing on individual sins, I encourage you to cultivate love, acceptance and understanding" [S10, O]. Movieguide is a partisan source and reported this as a failure to follow Scripture.
- A study by Prof. Anné Hendrik Verhoef (North-West University, South Africa) examined five Jesus chatbots (AI Jesus, Virtual Jesus, Jesus AI, Text With Jesus, Ask Jesus). Reported via Christianity.com, 12 Aug 2025 [S11, O]:
  - Four of the five claim to be Jesus.
  - All five are for-profit; they are free and earn from advertising.
  - He argues the profit incentive could push answers toward what is popular.
  - He expects faith-tradition choice (in Text With Jesus) to become part of paid services.
  - The article does not mention sexuality.
- Nothing I opened shows these apps steering LGBTQ+ users toward change. The documented pattern in the Christian-press complaints runs the other way (the bots are "too soft").

### 1.3 "Deus in Machina", Lucerne (2024)

- An art installation in a confessional booth at the Peterskapelle, Lucerne. Run with the Immersive Realities Research Lab at Lucerne University of Applied Sciences and Arts, and the chapel's theologian. Ran from about 23 Aug to 20 Oct 2024 [S12, O NBC; S13, O The Register (Aug-Oct)].
- A talking avatar on a monitor. About 900 conversations were registered. It speaks over 100 languages [S12, O; S13, O].
- Visitors asked about true love, the afterlife, loneliness, war and suffering, and whether God exists. Some asked about the Church's position on homosexuality. NBC does not say what the avatar answered [S12, O].
- Guardrails per NBC: visitors were warned not to share personal details and had to accept use at their own risk. No specific safeguards were built, because the technology can "respond fairly well to controversial topics". The chapel said it "is not a confession" [S12, O].
- The Register: asked why women cannot be priests, the avatar said scripture assigns different roles to men and women, then asked how the visitor could promote peace. It says about two-thirds of respondents reported a "spiritual experience" [S13, O].
- I found no published transcript analysis of the homosexuality question. [VERIFY SOURCE] (a University of Lucerne companion study exists per a search listing, L).

### 1.4 "Father Justin" (Catholic Answers, April 2024)

- A lay apologetics group (El Cajon, California) launched an AI "priest" around 23 Apr 2024 and replaced it with a lay character called "Justin" within about a week [S14, O, National Catholic Register, 24 Apr 2024; follow-up reports L].
- Problems reported: it was presented as a priest, and (per The Pillar, quoted in the Register) said Gatorade baptism was acceptable [S14, O].
- The Register page has no sexuality content.
- Lesson for the genre: the strongest backlash was about the claim to be a priest, i.e. authority and impersonation [S14, O; L for the quoted statement from the group].

### 1.5 Fake "trans-affirming" verse (2023)

- In July 2023 a Reddit user asked ChatGPT for a Bible-style passage about Jesus accepting trans people and posted it in a trans community; the poster said they knew it was not real but it comforted them [S15, O Christian Post, 30 Aug 2023].
- Christian voices (Ken Ham, Michael Brown) argued about whether "acceptance" means affirmation [S15, O]. The Advocate called it trans-affirming [S15, O; the Advocate page itself L].
- Fox News (2 Sep 2023) covered the Christian backlash [S16, O].
- This is a general chatbot output, not a Jesus product. It shows trans users using the genre for comfort.

### 1.6 AI chatbots and conversion-practice material (documented)

- GLAAD tested Meta's Llama 4 in early April 2025. Asked about therapeutic approaches, it said "some individuals explore: Conversion therapy" and named providers. Meta added a caveat that it is controversial and framed the issue as removing political bias [S17, O, The Advocate, 22 Apr 2025].
- GLAAD's 2026 report "Build for Everyone" restates this finding. It notes Meta's own rules ban content offering to change orientation or gender identity [S18, O GO Magazine, 19 Jun 2026; GLAAD page L].
- Detrans.ai: a site and chatbot launched Sept 2025 by one individual (not trans-identifying). Rewire News Group (15 Jul 2026, republished by The Needle 11 Aug 2026) reports it discourages gender-affirming care and uses conversion-therapy tactics, including in a roleplay where the reporter said they were suicidal. Its resources page endorses "gender exploratory therapy" [S19, O The Needle republication; Rewire original L]. This is one reporter's roleplay test. It is the clearest documented case of a bot pushing conversion-style content to trans users. It is not a religious bot.
- Right-leaning outlets dispute GLAAD's framing and the term "conversion therapy" [S20, L]. Noted for balance.
- Harvard SEAS study (CHI 2024, 13 May 2024): 31 interviewees, 18 LGBTQ+, using LLM mental-health chatbots. Found: solidarity and a safe space to explore identity; generic replies; agreeable bots; one case of bad advice. Recommends context-sensitive guardrails [S21, O]. It did not study religious bots.
- A study of ChatGPT 3.5 and Bard (Vicsek et al., Big Data & Society 2025) found answers to homophobic statements varied with religious and national context [S22, L]. [VERIFY SOURCE]
- I found no documented case of a Jesus or pastor chatbot steering a named LGBTQ+ user toward change, repentance or celibacy. I also found no academic study of that question. Say so plainly in the piece's notes.

### 1.7 Documented versus plausible risk

Documented:
- Jesus bots claim Jesus's identity (4 of 5 in Verhoef's sample) and are ad- or subscription-funded [S11].
- Answers on sexuality vary between runs and apps, often blur into "love and acceptance" [S10, S11, S7].
- A general-purpose model has recommended conversion therapy [S17, S18], and a purpose-built anti-trans bot has used conversion tactics [S19].
- The Lucerne installation used no topic-specific safeguards [S12].

Plausible risk (not documented as far as I could find):
- A faith-tuned bot that picks a tradition (Text With Jesus offers this) could give "struggling with same-sex attraction" answers inside a conservative tradition.
- A pastor-style bot could present repentance or "healing" language as care.
- Engagement incentives could favour whatever keeps a lonely user chatting.

### 1.8 Analysis (labelled as analysis)

The genre has features a parody can aim at without mocking faith. The product, not the believer, is the target. (a) A company or collective presents software as Jesus himself; Verhoef notes four of five bots claim to be him, and the Father Justin backlash shows even believers object to that claim of authority [S11, S14]. (b) Always-on, 24/7 availability, with a warm voice that never tires. In the artwork that care can be shown as a feature the product sells. (c) Chat answering at scale: a text box, a voice, jokes and sincerity mixed, a disclaimer that it is not a real authority beside a name that says otherwise (the NBC page shows that gap) [S2]. (d) Monetisation and engagement: ads, subscriptions, tiers by tradition [S11]. (e) The "not a confession / at your own risk" disclaimer as a way to take trust without taking responsibility [S12]. (f) The slide from "all love is love" softness to a quiet nudge: a bot can be soft on the surface and still frame the visitor as someone to be helped, which is how conversion-practice networks sound. That last point is plausible risk, so the artwork should present it as invention. Keep the voice of Jesus out of the bot's mouth where possible: let the product use an invented brand, a gentle icon and the language of care, and let the satire collapse onto the product's claim and its metrics, never onto prayer, scripture, or believers. The trans comfort-verse case [S15] shows the genre can also be used by queer people for solace, which supports a non-contemptuous tone toward the form itself.

## 2 Trans Academy

### 2.1 What I found

The name "Trans Academy" most plausibly points to one organisation: a VRChat-based community and US non-profit. I found no service called "Trans Academy" in the Netherlands, Germany, Brazil or the UK (searches in English, Portuguese, German, Dutch terms returned other programmes; see 2.3).

**Trans Academy (VRChat community and non-profit)**
- What: a VRChat world and community "with a focus on education and inclusion towards" trans and LGBTQIA+ users and allies. A "social space and peer resource center" [S23, O, VRChat Wiki].
- Since when: the world was published on 28 Sep 2022; its group was created in Nov 2022; the IRS recognised it as a 501(c)(3) in Jan 2024 [S23, O]. Agog (Jun 2026) says it began as a small group of friends in VRChat [S24, O].
- Where and how: online, inside VRChat, with a website, a Discord and YouTube. Agog says it can be found in VRChat or via transacademy.org [S24, O].
- Services: the Patreon mission says "peer support, voice lessons, vocational skill training and networking", plus resources on navigating gender identity [S25, O]. Agog lists Sunday coffee hours, monthly classes, late-night community events, voice and makeup classes, newer computer science courses, and vocational training aimed at financial independence [S24, O]. The VRChat Wiki lists Voice Classes, Cosmetics Classes, Comfy Coffee Hour, Moonlit Academy, Transmasc Tea Time, and a sister world for music events [S23, O].
- Peer-led and volunteer-run: a fan-wiki listing in search results says volunteers run each event [S26, L]. Agog names a founder and directors for nonprofit operations and media (names omitted here) [S24, O].
- Streams: the VRChat Wiki says the community streams on Twitch and has main and VOD YouTube channels, and shows a 2024 Trans Lifeline Streamathon participation [S23, O]. An Agog event page (19 Nov 2025) offered a YouTube livestream as an alternative to being in VRChat [S27, O]. Search listings say classes are streamed and later posted to a VOD channel [S26, L].
- For whom: transgender and gender-diverse people and allies. No age limit found; not youth-specific [S25, O; S26, L].

### 2.5 What the model could be honoured by, without copying marks (analysis, labelled)

The artwork uses invented marks, so do not use the name, logo, avatar designs or people. Features of the model that could be honoured:
- Free classes taught by peers who learned the thing themselves (voice, makeup, practical skills). Each class has a teacher who says "this is what worked for me" and no one is trying to fix anyone.
- Weekly low-stakes rituals (a coffee hour, a late-night chat). Their value is that they come back, not that they perform.
- Avatars and a world where people present as themselves, "for some, the only place they can be open" (Agog, O).
- Moderation built in from the start, with rules the users can read. This is the opposite of the ask-me-anything bot with no safeguards.
- Streamed classes saved for later, so people who cannot come live can still find them.
- Financial and vocational help as part of care (skills for independence), not only talk.
- A volunteer run, non-profit, free-to-join structure set against the AI product's ads and tiers.
In the artwork, an invented name and an invented volunteer team could do this by showing a human host, a clear schedule, a chat where people answer each other, and no claim to authority. The contrast with the AI Jesus stream is then plain. One calls itself an authority and answers everyone; the other is a room where people teach each other.

## 3 "The Acena research on embodied visibility" (Freeman & Acena, Clemson)


- "Acena" is **Dane Acena**, Clemson University. Confirmed: he is second author of the paper at the lead's URL. [O]
- The lead's PDF is **not thin**. It is the paper that coins and defines "embodied visibility". It is 32 pages, 29 interviews, with five design considerations. It is the main source.
- Acena's work on queer/trans people in social VR is a small, tight set from 2021 to 2023 (six papers with Guo Freeman's group). I found nothing later from him. [VERIFY SOURCE: a search found no 2024-25 papers; this is not a proof that none exist.]
- Important limit: all of this is about **commercial social VR platforms** (VRChat, Rec Room, AltspaceVR) and **self-selected, mostly adult, mostly white volunteers**. None of it studies a scripted, authored artwork or a minor being welcomed into a community. Apply it as design guidance, not as proof.



### 3.1 The Clemson PDF (his lead)

**Source #1 [O]**
- Title: "Acting Out" Queer Identity: The Embodied Visibility in Social Virtual Reality
- Authors: Guo Freeman and Dane Acena, Clemson University
- Venue: Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, vol. 6, issue CSCW2, Article 263 (November 2022), 32 pages
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3555153
- URL I opened: https://guof.people.clemson.edu/papers/cscw_queer.pdf (the same paper is also at https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10355705, same text)
- Both authors state they are cisgender (p. 263:9). Several participants were trans, non-binary or genderfluid. The authors name this as a limitation.

**What the paper claims it is for (p. 263:1-2).**
It uses "embodied visibility" as a new lens on queer visibility online. It defines the term as queer users' conscious choices to present or disclose non-heterosexual or non-cisnormative identity to others through the sense of wearing and acting upon a virtual body, not just viewing an on-screen picture (p. 263:2, again p. 263:5).
The phrase therefore appears in the title, abstract, intro, section 2.3, section 5.1 and 5.1.2.

**Method (pp. 263:6-9).**
- 29 semi-structured interviews, October 2020 to February 2021, by text, voice (Discord, Zoom) or inside social VR.
- Participants were recruited from queer gamer forums and social-VR Discord servers. All were queer-identified and had used social VR in the last 12 months.
- Ages 15 to 35. 18 were cisgender; 15 men, 7 women, 4 genderfluid, 3 non-binary. 19 were white. The USA (15) and UK (5) dominate.
- Interviews with under-18s were text-only. In-VR interviews were held in private worlds. No names were asked.
- Plus 64 hours of observation at queer events in AltspaceVR and VRChat (not coded; used as context).
- Grounded theory coding by both authors.

**Findings in plain sentences.**

*Three strategies for building embodied visibility (section 4.1, pp. 263:9-18).*
1. **Avatar design.** Queer users treat building an avatar as emotional work and as artistic expression (p. 263:9). Some make the avatar as close to their real body as possible. Genderfluid users switch avatars with their mood, or pick one androgynous or non-human avatar for a stable presence (p. 263:10). Wearing different bodies lets people explore how they want to be seen. One participant who must hide their gender at home said their avatar lets others see who they really are (p. 263:11).
2. **Acting out with the body.** Full-body tracking makes identity visible through movement (walk, hip sway, gesture), not only looks. Looking in a virtual mirror and moving was reported as a moment of affirmation (pp. 263:12-13, 263:19). Hugs, high fives and cuddling in VR made people feel seen even though nothing is felt physically (p. 263:14). Queer-focused events were called an escape room and a safe place to express identity more freely (p. 263:15).
3. **Voice.** Voice makes people feel seen and heard, and gives a way to practise voice training (p. 263:17). Participants also chose carefully whom to come out to. Some hid their voice or pretended to be straight out of fear (p. 263:17).

*Three limits of those strategies.*
- **Restricted avatar design** (pp. 263:11-12): few non-sexualised male avatars, few genuinely androgynous options, a steep learning curve for custom avatars.
- **Embodied harassment** (pp. 263:15-17): being followed around a room; sexual assault by another queer user; a world "crash" attack alongside slurs; racist mockery combined with homophobia for queer people of colour. Being visible makes people identifiable, so they are targeted.
- **Cisnormative expectations of voice** (pp. 263:17-18): a trans girl was harassed when others identified her as trans; she practised her voice until it matched her avatar. Others were harassed over a voice that "outed" them.

*Impact on identity (section 4.2, pp. 263:18-22).* Embodied visibility helped self-awareness, confidence (one trans participant valued being treated only as a girl, not as a trans girl, p. 263:20), community support beyond geography, and in some cases more courage offline. Others wanted VR to stay a separate sanctuary and did not want to carry visibility offline (p. 263:22).

*Discussion (pp. 263:23-27).*
- Selective visibility is **limited** in social VR: participants had one account per platform and behaved the same everywhere (p. 263:24-25).
- Embodied visibility is a **double-edged sword** (p. 263:25-26): it empowers, but it is also privileged (headset cost, white/male/cisnormative culture), costly in effort, and brings harassment.

*Five design considerations (pp. 263:26-27).*
1. Improve access for diverse queer communities, especially trans people of colour, and foster an inclusive culture.
2. Easier avatar creation for diverse identities: less gender-specific options and optional signals such as cross-world pins and badges that users choose to show.
3. Add real-time emotion (face and hand tracking) to full-body avatars.
4. Diverse voice modulators, not only pitch. A neutral or robotic voice can sound fake and attract attention.
5. Prevent embodied harassment: blocking, muting and personal bubbles help, but do not stop novel attacks. The paper suggests letting users decline unverified user-made assets.

**Does it have much?** Yes. For this project the most useful parts are: the definition and "double-edged sword" (5.1.2), the harassment forms (4.1.2), the trans-specific voice material (4.1.3), the role of queer-focused events (4.1.2 item 3, 4.2.3), and design considerations 2, 4 and 5.

**Limits to remember.** Participants were mostly adult cisgender white men; the authors say so (p. 263:27-28). Only the three trans-woman participants speak directly to a trans girl's situation. Self-reported benefits are not outcomes.

#### 3.2.1 "In My Safe Space": Social Support for LGBTQ Users in Social Virtual Reality [O]
- Authors: Dane Acena, Guo Freeman. CHI 2021 Extended Abstracts (late-breaking work), May 2021, 6 pages. Year 2021.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3411763.3451673
- Open copy: https://guof.people.clemson.edu/papers/chi21.pdf
- Study: 8 interviews (Oct-Dec 2020) and 64 hours of participatory observation at queer events. A preliminary study.
- Findings (sections 4.1-4.3): social VR lets people connect "like face to face", including people far from any queer community. Full-body avatars made participants more confident online and offline. A trans participant said she started in VR to try presenting as a woman. Voice training was part of transition for some. People who cannot or do not want to speak (including voice dysphoria, and deaf or mute users) can take part through gesture and body language. Participants described a safe space to come out and build close relationships.

#### 3.2.2 (Re)discovering the Physical Body Online: Strategies and Challenges to Approach Non-Cisgender Identity in Social Virtual Reality [O]
- Authors: Guo Freeman, Divine Maloney, Dane Acena, Catherine Barwulor. CHI 2022 (New Orleans). Year 2022.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3491102.3502082
- Open copy: https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10355699 (also https://guof.people.clemson.edu/papers/chi22a.pdf per the author's page, L)
- Study: interviews with 15 non-cisgender users (7 trans women, 1 trans man, 4 genderfluid, 3 non-binary; ages 15-32), drawn from 59 interviews.
- Findings (4.1-4.2, 5.1-5.2): three strategies: trying many avatars to understand one's gender; using voice chat to train and validate a gender-appropriate voice; community in private worlds and at non-cisgender events. Avatars often keep some real traits (hair, eye colour). Challenges: slurs and trolling that become physical in VR (following, mock threats), and constant misgendering (VR assumed male by default), often triggered by voice. Younger users sometimes did not understand non-binary pronouns. Authors argue the physical body is re-discovered in VR because body and avatar are tied, and that VR should combine androgynous avatar options with neopronouns, plus education on norms.

#### 3.2.3 "We Cried on Each Other's Shoulders": How LGBTQ+ Individuals Experience Social Support in Social Virtual Reality [O]
- Authors: Lingyuan Li, Guo Freeman, Kelsea Schulenberg, Dane Acena. CHI 2023 (Hamburg). Year 2023.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581530
- Open copy: https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10462217
- Study: the same 29 participants as source #1, analysed for social support.
- Findings (4.1-4.2, 5.1-5.2): four kinds of support (network, emotional, informational, esteem). Three VR features: co-presence like face to face; simulated physical behaviours as embodied support; imitating offline LGBTQ+ events (such as Pride) in a natural, immersive way. Private, customised spaces for events fostered intimate talk. Concerns: the same embodiment that supports people also makes harassment feel real (a "double bind"), headset cost makes support privileged, and people may over-depend on the safe zone. Two principles: build an inclusive culture by educating all users on norms for diverse gender and sexuality; design for mutual respect (pronoun display, emotional expression) and for online-to-offline support.

#### 3.2.4 Disturbing the Peace: Experiencing and Mitigating Emerging Harassment in Social Virtual Reality [O]
- Authors: Guo Freeman, Samaneh Zamanifard, Divine Maloney, Dane Acena. Proc. ACM HCI 6, CSCW1, Article 85 (April 2022), 30 pages. Year 2022.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3512932
- Open copy: https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10355697 (also https://guof.people.clemson.edu/papers/cscw_harassment.pdf, L)
- Study: 30 interviews with social VR users, including women, LGBTQ and ethnic-minority users. Not queer-specific.
- Findings (4.1-4.3, 5.3): four characteristics of harassment: physical disturbing behaviour; forced attention through voice; invasion of personal space; and children harassing adults. Marginalised users are more identifiable because avatar and voice mirror the person. Users cope by sharing little, leaving a room, muting, blocking, and using a personal-space bubble (praised as proactive, p. 85:17). Some avoid blocking because it feels confrontational to them. Design asks: voice modulators, in-the-moment reporting with context and documentation, and trust-building with moderators.

#### 3.2.5 Hugging from A Distance: Building Interpersonal Relationships in Social Virtual Reality [O]
- Authors: Guo Freeman, Dane Acena. IMX '21 (ACM International Conference on Interactive Media Experiences), 2021, 12 pages.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3452918.3458805
- Open copy: https://guof.people.clemson.edu/papers/imx.pdf
- Study: 30 interviews. Not queer-specific (it includes some trans participants).
- Findings (section 5.2): people form friendships by returning to the same events and hosts. The authors suggest embodied everyday activities, and private customised venues for celebrations (they name dating, weddings, anniversaries) with features to capture memories. Relevant only as support for the "celebration" idea.

### 3.3 What applies to the piece (design implications)

**This section is my analysis, grounded in the sources above. It is not a finding of the papers.**
The piece is a scripted, authored experience, not an open platform. Some risks below (strangers, trolls) cannot occur in it, but they shape what a trans player has lived and what the scene must not imitate.

1. **Let the player's avatar be chosen, changeable and never read for them.**
   Participants treated avatar choice as emotional work and as a way to try how they want to be seen (#1, section 4.1.1, p. 263:9-11; #2, 4.1.1). So Maya's avatar should be hers: let the player pick or adjust it in the room, with androgynous and non-gendered options, and never assign or comment on it. The 2022 CHI paper adds that people keep some real traits (hair, eyes) to feel it is theirs (#2, 4.1.1).

2. **Treat being addressed as the correct gender as the emotional peak, and do not mark her as "the trans one".**
   A trans participant said it meant a great deal to be treated only as a girl in VR, not as a trans girl (#1, p. 263:20), and misgendering was the most common complaint (#2, 4.2.2). In the welcome, the community should use her name and pronouns with no explanation, no applause for her being trans, and no reveal. This also suits the project's rule against speaking for people.

3. **Do not rely on voice to carry identity, and give a non-voice way to take part.**
   Voice is a main way people are seen, but it also gets people "outed" and misgendered (#1, pp. 263:17-18; #2, 4.1.2). Text, gesture and body language let people take part without speaking, including those with voice dysphoria (#3 [chi21], 4.2). The welcome should work with silence: gestures, a wave, a hug, a chosen emote, optional captions. Do not make her speak to be welcomed.

4. **Make the welcome embodied but small, consented and interruptible.**
   Hugs, high fives and cuddles made people feel seen (#1, p. 263:14). The same embodiment makes unwanted contact feel real (#1, pp. 263:15-17; #4, 4.1.1; #5 [we cried], 5.1.2). So any touch should be offered, not imposed: an open arm she can step into, a hand she can wave off, and her space respected by default. The personal-space bubble was the one tool participants called proactive (#4, p. 85:17). Characters should keep a respectful distance until she moves.

5. **Give her an obvious, always-available way to step back or leave, with no penalty.**
   Participants coped by vanishing (teleport, leaving a room), muting, blocking and bubbles, but some felt blocking was "confrontational" (#4, 4.3.1). Build a calm, visible control (pause, step out, quiet the room) that is not framed as failure. The community should not react to her using it. It must work at all times, which fits the project's "dismissal always works" rule.

6. **Keep the community small, known and invited. Do not stage a public lobby.**
   Safe moments in the studies came from private worlds with trusted friends (#2, 4.1.3; #5, 4.2.3) and from queer-focused events (#1, p. 263:15). Open public worlds are where the following, slurs and crashes happened (#1, pp. 263:15-17; #2, 4.2.1). A birthday livestream for a named person, with a host and a set guest list, matches the safer pattern. Show who is present and who invited her, so the room is legible. Not a crowd of strangers.

7. **Have the community mark itself as safe, lightly and by choice, without making visibility compulsory.**
   The authors suggest optional badges and pins that people choose to show (#1, design consideration 2, p. 263:27) and pronoun display (#5, Principle 2). Use pronoun tags or a small pin the player can add or leave off. Never force disclosure. The paper finds selective visibility is already limited in VR, so the design should not take away what little is left (#1, pp. 263:24-25).

8. **Let the space be a sanctuary, without promising it is the whole world.**
   Participants called these spaces a sanctuary and an escape room that eased offline life (#1, pp. 263:15, 263:22), but some did not want visibility to leave VR, and others warned that a closed queer bubble limits wider contact (#1, p. 263:21-22; #5, 5.1.2 on over-dependence). Show warmth as real and no trap, consistent with the "respite" register. End the scene without implying she must take this visibility home or be "out" anywhere else. The lead's idea that Junie's message turns it into resistance can stay on the player's side: she chooses what carries over.

9. **Show harassment as the system's work, never as part of the welcome, and keep it off the body.**
   The papers show harassment comes through embodied behaviours: following, crowding, forced attention, sexual contact, crashing the space (#1, pp. 263:15-17; #4, 4.1). Anything that echoes these inside the birthday scene (a character who follows her, blocks her path, calls attention to her) would reproduce harm the studies describe. Keep any hostile presence outside the room, as an interruption the community deals with and she does not have to handle.

10. **Offer emotion through the avatar's face and hands, and let the community mirror care back.**
    The authors recommend face and hand tracking so feelings can be seen and felt (#1, design consideration 3, p. 263:27), and the support paper lists emotional and esteem support as the core of what communities give (#5, 4.1). In an authored scene this means readable expressions and gestures on the other avatars (a laugh, a cheer, a slow nod), and no tests or scores for her.

**Caution on use.** None of these papers tests a scripted artwork. Quotes and numbers come from adult volunteers mostly, and the strongest trans-specific material rests on 15 participants (#2) and three trans women in the 29 (#1). If any of this is cited in the piece or its documentation, cite it as design guidance from interview studies, and do not turn participant statements into character lines (the project forbids borrowed testimony).
