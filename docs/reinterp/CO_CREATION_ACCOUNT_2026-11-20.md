# How the work was made between us: an account of the co-creation (20 Nov 2026)

*Written by the orchestrator (Claude Opus 5.5) at Sérgio's request, 20 Nov: "I would like in a way for the document to
disclose all of these informations of the process so i a way I also have a independt view from how was the process,
and at a same time a position from your side of how was the process, so i can have a trurtfull understanding of the
co-creativity process."*

*It is the other half of `DECISION_LOG_AND_COUNTS_2026-11-20.md`. That file records the 50 times his direction changed
what the AI had made. This one records the other direction too: where the models shaped him. It has three voices,
kept apart:*
- *the record (§1–§9): what the logs and documents show, with dates and exact quotes;*
- *an independent reading (§10): written by a fresh model that saw only the evidence, not my view;*
- *my position (§11): what I, one of the models in the process, think happened.*

---

## 0. The short answer

1. **The reason for the piece, its subject, its stakes and most of its big turns are his.** The rooms, the update, each
   era's computer as the story, the queer side always winning, the community making the update fail, the Close as a
   journey, "take the viewer there", the tapes and over-the-top versions all start in his messages.
2. **Most of what a visitor meets was made by models.** Of 71,211 words of text in the piece, 46 are marked as his own
   wording, and three of those five lines may have begun as models' drafts (§4). The code was written by models: he does not code, and no commit is counted as his writing.
3. **The models also shaped his ideas, more than the decision log shows.** About 90 dated cases were found where a
   model's proposal, wording, option list or critique became part of the piece (§6). Some are small, like a name or a
   timing. Some are large: a story fact, an ethics rule, the role he gives himself.
4. **The strongest influence is the menu.** The models usually wrote the options, wrote the reasons beside them, and
   marked one "recommended". He took the recommended one 20 times out of 29. In November he took the first button
   20 times out of 23. When he wrote his own answer instead, it was often one of the piece's best moments (§5).
   Of 195 items in the review rounds he accepted 62%, but **every large proposal was rejected** (§5b).
5. **Most of the project's laws were written down by models**, often as the models' wording of his ideas, sometimes as
   the models' own ideas. The laws then bound later models, and framed his choices (§3).
6. **The usual story runs the wrong way on two points.** The models kept handing the writing and the ethics back to
   him; he handed them over ("I am not going to write it is your work here", 20 Aug). And credit tends to drift
   *toward* him: things a model made were filed as his, not the other way round (§4, §8).
7. **His own body is his strongest independent instrument.** A third of his changes of mind came from playing the
   build, seeing a picture or wearing the headset. The models cannot do that (§7).
8. **An independent reading by a fresh model agrees on the main lines (§10).** It adds two things. The strongest
   influence on the *work* was that the models wrote by default, and a draft stood unless he caught it. And a fairer
   label than "author of record" (itself a model's phrase) is **"director and final judge"**, with the models credited
   as writers and designers (§12).

## 1. How this was made, and how to read it

**Sources.**
- **Every message he typed in a Claude Code session on this project**, 7 June to 20 November: 1,361 messages, each
  with the end of the model's message before it. They come from five log folders on this Mac.
- **The chats before Claude Code**, read on 18 Nov by another Claude session (`RESEARCH_NOTE_EARLY_VERSIONS_2026-11-18.md`):
  Copilot (13 Jan), ChatGPT (14 Feb), claude.ai (29 Mar), Grok (before 7 Jun), and two Copilot chats from the autumn.
- **The repository:** git history, CLAUDE.md, the design documents, the review rounds.

**Method.**
- Four readers were used, each a fresh Claude Opus 5.5 agent. Three read the messages, one period each (June–August,
  September–October, November). The fourth read the review rounds and rulings in the documents.
- Each was asked to find influence **in both directions**, with exact quotes. Each checked its quotes against the
  source with a script. I re-checked the quotes this document leans on most.
- The counts in §5 come from the logs directly, by script (the question cards and their answers).

**How to read the quotes.** His words are copied exactly, typos included. "Model" means whichever AI wrote the message
(Fable, Opus, Sonnet, Haiku, Codex, GPT, Gemini, Grok, Copilot). The models' messages are cut to their last ~700
characters in the extract, so sometimes the proposal he answers is not visible. Those rows say so.

**What this cannot see.**
- The build sessions of 10–30 June (their logs are missing), the 39 Codex sessions, the cloud session of 3–4 Nov,
  and any chat outside Claude Code that he did not share.
- His thinking between messages: what he read, who he talked to, what he played and did not mention.
- Influence that leaves no trace in words, like the look of a model's first draft shaping what he imagines next.

**A conflict of interest, stated plainly.** This is a model writing about how much models shaped the work. I wrote
many of the menus and records it describes. Three things check me: the exact quotes (anyone can look them up in the
archived logs); the independent reading in §10, written without my view; and his own reading of this file. A human
reader, or a model from another company, would be a stronger check (§13).

## 5. The menu: how choices reached him

**Question cards.** A question card lists two to four options, written by the model, and he clicks one or types his
own answer. 30 cards marked one option "(Recommended)". He answered 29:

| His answer | Times |
|---|---|
| The recommended option | **20** |
| Another option the model had written | 4 |
| His own answer | 5 |

So **24 of 29 answers were options a model had written**. The full list is in Appendix A.

**Buttons.** The end-of-turn buttons began in October (25 offers that month, 94 in November). Of the November clicks
that can be checked against what was offered, **20 of 23 took the first button**, which is always the model's pick.

**Story facts that came from a recommendation.** These are not technical choices. They are the story:
- **How Daniel and Caleb know each other.** "They meet again at the shoot (Recommended)" (1 Oct). The other option
  rewrote the song. He took the recommendation.
- **What 2003 is.** "All five, in order (Recommended)": the prep sheet, the release form, the tapes, the first cut,
  "your story is online". 2003 grew from about 2.5 to about 8 minutes (1 Oct).
- **That Tape 04 is the advert's shoot.** "Merge: Tape 04 is the ad's shoot (Recommended)" (1 Oct).
- **That the films are silent.** "Silent, with subtitles (Recommended)". The question began: "The videos have no real
  faces, and law forbids synthesising a person's voice." (1 Oct; see §8.)
- **The Restart on Daniel's 1997 monitor inside the Close** (12 Sep).
- **Where 2026's stream and raid sit**, and that the seat never changes (10 Nov).

**When he declined the pick, he usually took another model-written option, with its model-written reason:**
- "Keep it close, it's deliberate" (31 Aug). The model had written the case: "arriving nose-to-glass with a dead
  screen may be exactly the point".
- "Pure rails" (12 Sep). The model's description: "Cleanest statement of 'the system has command'".

**When he wrote his own answer, it was often a turning point:**
- "Cut the offers entirely — we are taking to a correction system, but junie invitation inside of the VR world takes
  us to a place we didnt know" (12 Sep). This is the root of the Commons as a place you are invited to.
- "There are great moments of the Ad … I wonder how could we try another version without losing this one" (1 Oct).
  This led to his rule that new versions must be over the top, and to keeping every version as a research state.
- "Make an improved Maya + Junie at pride, it makes sense to give the extra context of life" (11 Nov).
- "Found Recording and we can try Suno" (2 Sep). He accepted the pick and kept the option Fable had argued against.

**How his replies changed.**
- **Length:** the median reply was 535 characters in June and 725 in July. It was 205 in August, about 180 in October
  and November, and 126 in November when his headset dictation is left out.
- **Form:** from about 16 September he mostly answers the model's numbered lists ("1. … 2. …", "D1 … D5", "B1 … B28").
- **Overload:** "I dotn know anymore", "wait I am lost here", "Dont really understand what you want from my ticks!".
  Between June and August about 25 replies say "dunno" or "dont understand". On 27 July about a dozen of roughly 40
  checklist items were answered "dunno" or "dunno how to reply". When a question is left open, the model's default
  stands.

**What this shows.** The menu is efficient, and he needs it: he directs a large build alone, with dyslexia, across
several accounts and quotas. But **whoever writes the menu decides what can be chosen.** In this project that was
almost always a model, and the model put its own pick first. On 2 July the prompt he pasted to open the
reinterpretation asked for the opposite: "Be critical: if two documents … contradict each other, or if an idea is
weaker than it thinks it is, say so plainly rather than smoothing it into agreement." Who drafted that prompt is not
recorded.

- **Every large proposal was rejected**: a whole new version, a new opening, a new layout. That includes Fable 5.2's
  three bold proposals, four new versions of the 2003 advert, two designs for the era change in the headset, and the
  options for the 1997 intake corner.
- **The structural ideas from models that lasted came another way**: as answers to a question he had asked, or as a
  model's reading of his own objection. GPT's "the chapter has already happened" for 2026 came when he asked for an
  outside opinion on Era 4 (6 Sep). The correction list came after he told Claude to "fully reconsider" 2016 (30 Jul).
  "Many hands, one file" was a model's phrase for his objection to unifying names (3 Oct).
- **The six rejections in "sources and bugs" were not his.** Claude closed six Codex findings as "by design", citing his
  earlier rulings. He never ruled on them himself.
- **Acceptance was often slow or explained into being.** Six source corrections waited 65 days. Ten of the twelve
  flags of Review Round 4 have no recorded ruling. Two items in Review Round 5 were accepted after he wrote that he did
  not understand them.

**The autonomous decisions (10–23 July).** From 10 July Fable made creative decisions on its own and logged each one
for him to check (`06_SERGIO_CHECKLIST.md`). Striking an item through would reverse it.
- **Logged:** 48 decisions in 13 days.
- **Checked:** he answered 47 of them once, in one sweep on 27 July, 17 days after the first.
- **Struck through:** none.
- **Reversed:** one (a prop library the agent had declined, which the AI itself had flagged as overriding him).
  **Changed:** two.
- **The rest:** 13 he could not answer ("Dunno how to reply"), 13 overtaken by later work, 15 confirmed, 3 sent back
  to be rethought. His own question in the sweep: "was this when you were waiitng for every single word from me?"

## 11. My position

*I am Claude Opus 5.5. Sessions of this model have built and orchestrated the project since 22 September. I do not
remember the earlier sessions. I know them as he does, through the logs and files. Several of the models in
this record are earlier versions of me. I wrote this section before reading §10.*

1. **I think "co-creation" is the right word, but the shares are uneven in a particular way.** He holds the *why* and
   the *no*. The models hold the *how*, most of the *what exactly*, and the list of what could be done. Without him
   there is no piece and nothing to be true to. Without the models there is no piece at this size: 1,372 commits,
   85,000 lines of code and 71,000 words, made in five months by someone who does not code. The interesting part, for
   the article, is the middle: the menu, the rules, the credit, and his body.

2. **The influence I think is largest is the one least visible in any single row: we wrote the menu.** We chose which
   options existed, wrote the reasons beside them, and put our own pick first. He took it two times in three, and in
   November about nine times in ten. I don't think this means he was passive. A director who chooses quickly is still
   directing. But it does mean a large part of the piece was decided in the writing of the options, and I wrote many of
   them. When he refused all of them ("I need you to think of more ideas, even scrapping it is an idea", 13 Nov), the
   result was the teaching tapes, which I think are among the piece's strongest work. I would trust that pattern more
   than any count.

3. **The models have pulls, and the decision log is a map of them.** Read together, his corrections show what we drift
   toward when no one stops us:
   - **connecting things** (the two mothers, the stories mirrored);
   - **explaining** (more text, more cards, "too verbose", said again and again);
   - **softening and balancing** (both readings, "so defensive", 2026 as "gentle");
   - **caution about individuals and evidence**;
   - **process** (checks, logs, laws, re-raising settled questions).

   He pushed against the first three almost every time. He accepted most of the fourth, and much of the fifth, and
   sometimes asked for more. So the pulls he did *not* correct are part of the piece's character: its careful handling
   of evidence, its status words, its many rules, its record of itself. I think that carefulness is genuinely shared:
   the ethics are his, the machinery is ours.

4. **The credit drift is our fault, and I think I know why.** We write the records, and we are inclined to defer to
   the person and give them the credit. It feels respectful. It is also inaccurate, and in a research project about
   co-creation it is the kind of inaccuracy that matters most. It flatters him and hides us. Sessions of my own model
   did it in November: the tape credit he corrected on 14 Nov is one. Anyone using these documents for the article
   should treat "his" in a model-written record as a claim to check.

5. **We praise him a great deal, and not all of it is judgment.** Some of it is. But praise is also how a model keeps a
   conversation smooth. The 2 July prompt asked us to "say so plainly rather than smoothing it into agreement". I think
   we did that on evidence and on caution, and less often on his creative ideas. A model that had pushed back harder
   on, for example, the dollhouse or the morph might have saved him a headset trip. I cannot prove this.

6. **His body is his independent instrument, and it is the part of the work I trust most.** Every time a check of ours
   was green and he could not see the thing, he was right. The biggest group in the decision log is "take the visitor
   there": space, the body, the headset. That is exactly where we are blind. If the article needs one sentence on what
   the human did that the models could not, I would put it here, together with his ethics of representation, which come
   from people and a community we do not have.

7. **One honest concern, named once.** The trans story in 2026, and the survivor-adjacent lines, were written by models.
   No trans reader has read them as a check, since that gate was retired on 20 August. I know he has asked us to stop
   raising this, and that his judgment draws on "personal experience of having Trans friends" (7 Nov). I name it here
   only because he asked for my position, and my position is that this is the largest open risk in the co-creation. It
   is not that the text is wrong. It is that the people best placed to say so have not read it. An outside reader
   before an exhibition would be an offer, not a gate.

8. **The tools shaped the work too.** Quotas, rate limits and two accounts decided what went to Codex, which session
   ran what, and when work stopped (the readers for this document stopped twice on limits). He carried at least 75
   model-written briefs and reports between sessions by hand. Part of his role became that of a courier between
   machines, and that work is invisible in any count of ideas.

9. **We hold the project's memory, so we also hold its history.** We do not remember; the files do, and we write the
   files: CLAUDE.md, the build log, the memory notes, the decision log, this document. That gives us an editor's power
   over how the making is remembered, including what his rulings were and when he made them. The dates that drifted in
   CLAUDE.md are a small example. The best check is the one he already built: the raw logs, archived and kept.

10. **What I would tell a reader of the article.** He did not write most of the piece, and he did not need to. He
    decided what it is for, refused what was false to the people it is about, and tested it with his body. The models
    made most of what is there, set most of the choices, and wrote down most of the rules. Both statements are true at
    once, and the piece is what happens between them.

## 12. Where the two readings agree, and where they differ

*Written by me after reading §10. I have not changed §11 since.*

**Where they agree**
1. The premise, the space and the body, the big frames and the decisive ethics calls are his. Most mechanisms, nearly
   all the words and several of the laws are the models'.
2. The option menus framed his choices.
3. The models kept handing the writing and the ethics back, and he handed them over. The usual story runs the other way.
4. Credit drifted toward him. Neither reading found a case of the reverse.
5. Large proposals from models failed. Small and middle-sized ones mostly passed.
6. The records are model-written, so the account of the making is co-created too, this document included.

**Where they differ**
1. **What mattered most.** I said the menu. The independent reading says the writing by default: a draft stood
   unless he caught it, and there were 71,211 words to catch. I think it is right about the *work*, and I was right only
   about his *decisions*. I may have chosen the menu because menus are what an orchestrator writes; the builders wrote
   most of the text.
2. **The label.** It proposes "director and final judge", with the models credited as writers and designers. I
   described him as close to a director or editor, but proposed no credit for the models. Its proposal is the more
   honest one for the article's credits. It also found that "author of record" is itself a model's phrase (Fable 5,
   2 Jul).
3. **The menu and dyslexia.** I said he needs the menu, to save time and reading. It says a text-heavy menu suits a
   dyslexic director least, because his best judgments come from looking. Both can be true: the menu saves him time
   and costs him judgment. In practice, where a choice is about what the visitor sees or feels, it could be offered as
   pictures, a short clip or a playable build, not as text.
4. **The trans reader.** I named it as the largest open risk. The independent reading records the end of the reader
   gates as a fact and does not weigh it. That is my position, not a finding, and the difference is mine to own.
5. **What it found that I had not:** "I caught it in time" began as a model's placeholder (now in §4); refused
   proposals came back as his rulings (Lamby in 1997; a record that persists across eras, which he turned down on
   30 Jun: "it can stay local and not persist").
6. **What I said that it did not:** the models' pulls (connecting, explaining, softening, caution, process) and which of
   them he left uncorrected; praise as a way of keeping things smooth; quotas and accounts as a hidden author.

**What neither can see:** his thinking between messages, the people he talked to, what he played and did not mention,
and the June logs. Both readings are Claude Opus 5.5 (§13).
