# Dykas, Sexual Sanity for Women (2012): the model, the same-sex passages, the language of care

Research note, SurvivingSOGICE. Read-only; no project file was edited.

Source: local PDF `_OceanofPDF.com_Sexual_sanity_for_women_-_Ellen_Mary_Dykas.pdf` (212 PDF pages). Edition: *Sexual Sanity for Women: Healing from Relational and Sexual Brokenness*, Harvest USA, Ellen Dykas (editor), New Growth Press, Greensboro NC, copyright 2012, ISBN 978-1-938267-00-0. Page numbers are the book's printed numbers (PDF page minus 8 for most of the book). Text was extracted with macOS PDFKit. The note paraphrases throughout and quotes two short phrases in total (pp.41, 191).

## Summary

1. The book's model is the Harvest USA Tree Model: five parts (fruit, seed, roots, soil, shoots) that treat sexual and relational sin as the fruit of a heart shaped by its soil.
2. Female same-sex attraction gets one dedicated session (Session 6, pp.38-45). The book holds that attraction is not sin unless acted on, but that homosexuality is sexual sin and that a gay self-identity is a lie to be replaced.
3. Care is the dominant register (compassion, healing, change as a lifelong process, mutual confession), and correction runs through it: the heart is always the target and no end-state is defined.
4. The 20 sessions share one repeatable structure (opening concept, review, main exploration, applied reflection, homework). The My Tree sheet, the Carnes cycle, the Two Sides of Change chart and the Two Trees appendix are the most usable interface elements.
5. Three 2016 ideas are proposed: a My Tree intake that the system reflects back, a Two Trees sort that cannot be won, and an accountability habit log that never reaches complete.
6. Caveats: this is the 2012 edition only; the newer book *Sexual Wholeness for Women* is not in the file; several factual claims are flagged [VERIFY SOURCE]; testimonies are unattributed and were not quoted.

## The Model

- Where it appears: introduced in Session 1 (p.10) as a model for understanding behaviour, built on Jesus's teaching that a tree is known by its fruit (Matthew 12:33; Luke 6:43-45, pp.9-10). Explained in full in Session 2 (pp.13-20), titled Out of the Heart: The Tree Model. Applied to the reader in Session 3 (pp.21-22).
- The five parts (pp.14-18):
  - Fruit: the behaviours, thoughts and relational patterns the reader wants changed. Examples include envy, anger, gossip, sexual fantasy, pornography, promiscuity, masturbation and homosexual behaviour (p.15).
  - Seed: the heart, framed as the current condition of the heart given the question of who is lord, self or Christ (diagram, p.14). The sinful heart makes self the centre; the remedy is a new heart and repentance (pp.15-16).
  - Roots: God-given desires for relationship, intimacy, comfort and significance, which are bent toward self-serving satisfaction, called idolatry (p.16). Examples are a woman seeking male sexual attention for love, a woman enmeshed with another woman because it feels secure, and a woman using pornography as escape.
  - Soil: external influences the reader did not choose: temperament, culture, family dynamics, gender, body type, talents, abuse, trauma, biology and spiritual warfare (pp.16-17). Sessions 7-12 work through these.
  - Shoots: the beliefs or worldviews that act as lenses on life (pp.17-18). Sessions 14-15 cover them; Appendix E (pp.187-193) lists common lies behind them.
- How the reader uses it: complete the My Tree sheet (pp.20-21) with two or three details per part, then share in the group. Later sessions deepen each part; Session 20 returns to a personal Tree Review (p.163).
- Underlying logic: behaviour is symptom; the heart is the problem. The book says a programmatic approach to changing behaviour fails because behaviours are symptoms of a deeper problem (p.19). Change comes through repentance, grace and a renewed mind.
- Adjacent models the book uses:
  - The Carnes cycle of sexual addiction (Preoccupation, Ritualization, Compulsive Actions, Despair), attributed to the psychologist Patrick Carnes, with HALT triggers (hungry, angry, lonely, tired) (pp.125-127, Session 16).
  - Two Sides of Change: grace as passive change, works as active change (pp.130-131).
  - A Cycle of Abuse, credited to Sarah Lipp (Session 9).
  - The Two Trees of Codependency: two self-serving relational styles, one serving and one controlling, each with Heart Idol and Unbelief pairs (Appendix F, pp.195-196).

## Same-sex attraction and women

Coverage: the word homosexuality appears about 34 times in the text and same-sex about 19 times. The word lesbian appears once, inside a testimony (Appendix D, p.179). Bisexuality and transgender identity are not addressed as such. Gender is treated in Session 15 (pp.113-115) and in one Common Lies entry (p.188).

- Session 6, The Fruit: Understanding Female Homosexuality (pp.38-45). Key concept: women are made to be friends, sisters and mentors, not romantic partners or husband-substitutes, and the pull between women comes from the same heart as the pull toward men (p.38).
- Spectrum (p.40): from emotionally enmeshed bonds with a romantic or sensual feel (named idolatry) through to a gay identity and homosexual relationships. Both are presented as the same heart at work.
- Scripture summary, a numbered list (pp.40-42):
  - Homosexuality is sexual sin, since sexual activity belongs in marriage, defined as one woman and one man for life (p.41).
  - Emotional dependency and mental fantasy are idolatry, whatever the gender of the object (p.41).
  - The book says same-sex attraction "is not sin unless acted upon in thought and/or behavior" (p.41). This is its most permissive line.
  - A gay identity is described as a late-twentieth-century development (p.41). Labelling oneself by attraction is said to get around what Scripture says, and identity is said to come from one's relationship with God (p.42).
- Change (pp.42-43): the book concedes significant debate over whether change is possible. It says change is possible through Christ but that temptation will be lifelong. Some women shift toward men or toward one husband, others do not, and the goal is holiness rather than a generalised heterosexuality (p.43).
- Common Lies (pp.187-192): the belief that one is born gay is listed as a lie (p.191). The book states that "there is no conclusive evidence of a physiological, genetic cause of homosexuality" (p.191), calls simple born-this-way or just-a-choice accounts unhelpful, and attributes the particular sin to the heart (seed) responding to soil (pp.191-192). A related lie (p.188) joins a belief about identifying with the male gender to a self-definition as gay.
- Vignette (Session 2, pp.18-19): a hypothetical woman who identifies as gay, wants to obey Jesus, believes men are unsafe, and is still in contact with a recent girlfriend by social media and text. The group is asked what the first steps to help her would be. She exists only as a discussion prompt, but she is the book's clearest model of the case.
- Testimonies (Session 17, pp.141-143; Appendix D, pp.178-179): two first-person accounts by women who describe leaving same-sex attraction or relationships. They attribute the attraction to soil (family, trauma, anger, fear, gender detachment) and reframe attraction as admiration that became sexual. Both are unattributed. They are not quoted here (see Caveats).

## The language of care

The book's own register is pastoral and warm. The mechanisms below show how that register carries correction. The project's term for this is weaponised care. The evidence supports describing care as the channel and a fixed outcome as the content. Whether weaponised is the fair word is the lead's judgement.

1. Compassion as the entry point, diagnosis as the content. Jesus is presented as not condemning, but as focused on deeper motivational issues rather than behaviour (p.10). The compassion licenses going beneath behaviour to the heart, where the reader's own desires are the thing to correct. The book says desires should serve the reader rather than rule her, which places the desire itself under review (p.19).
2. Self-description disallowed. The book says the reader is not her sin and that her identity comes from God (pp.187-188, p.42). That reads as comfort. But the self-descriptions a same-sex woman might use are the specific things the book rejects (pp.41-42). The separation of person from sin is offered only where the self-description is disallowed.
3. Change defined by the book, then lengthened. The book says change is a process and that its definition can shift, so that earlier expectations may not be met (pp.2-3). If a reader does not change, the frame can read that as a failure of her expectations rather than of the method. Behaviour is treated as symptom (p.19), so the only available measure is the heart, which the group and its leader are placed to judge.
4. Confession and accountability as mutual oversight. The group guidelines name confessing weakness and sin as the group's strength, ask members to share enough to be known, forbid sharing anything outside the group, and route questions to the leader (pp.2-3). The community commitment asks members to ask hard questions of accountability (Appendix A, p.171). Session 19 lists loving, truth-speaking confession and correction among the marks of a godly friendship (pp.158-159). Care supplies the consent; the group supplies the enforcement.
5. Disclaimer of clinical role. The group is explicitly not a counselling service for trauma and abuse, and professional counselling is strongly recommended (p.2). The book nonetheless includes a trauma session (Session 9). The care is pastoral and the trauma is referred on, so responsibility for outcomes stays with the reader and her church.
6. Levelling as a rhetorical move. The book places sexual sins on the same level as gossip, stealing and anger (p.188). That reads as equal footing, but the same book singles out homosexuality for a definitional verdict (p.41) and asks for a different outcome from it.
7. Lie-and-truth replacement. The reader is asked to name the lies she believes and replace them with truths (p.187). Each entry pairs a self-understanding with a corrective. Care and correction are one act here.
8. Long-horizon hope as a standing condition. Lifelong temptation is presented as normal (p.43), the Christian life as a race (pp.163-164), and slow progress as the only victory on offer.

Render note for the piece: the book itself names significant debate over whether change is possible (p.42) and does not resolve it. Per the project's doctrine, both captions should stay open; the book's own text does not support a single verdict.

## Exercises and structures

- Session anatomy (about two hours): a welcome or review (15-20 min); the main exploration (60 min, sometimes 75); an applied section, Making It Personal (10-25 min); On Your Own (5 min). Each session opens with a boxed key concept and a prayer to open, and closes with group prayer (e.g. p.129).
- Discussion questions: numbered lists of five to nine per session, with sub-prompts. Many ask the reader to describe her own experience.
- Read-aloud passages, followed by discussion of how the passages correspond to each part of the model (Session 2, Q9, p.19).
- Journal prompts, plus an optional private prayer and processing journal (p.3).
- My Tree sheet (pp.20-21): five boxes. Fruit: behaviours God is changing. Shoots: the beliefs the reader is aware of now. Soil: influences on her life. Roots: family background, significant events, the messages heard about men, women and sex. Seed: how she would describe her heart, including ways she was sinned against sexually or relationally.
- Carnes stages with reflective questions about one's own stages (pp.125-127).
- Two Sides of Change chart (pp.130-131): a two-column comparison of grace and works.
- How Christ Changes Our Patterns of Response chart (Session 17, p.140), with actions under focus on Jesus, putting on Jesus, fleeing, seeking others' help, and setting the mind on higher things.
- Flee prompts (pp.140-141): which people, gatherings and social-media connections must be avoided.
- Common Lies, as lie and truth pairs (Appendix E, pp.187-193).
- Two Trees of Codependency, with Heart Idol and Unbelief pairs (Appendix F, pp.195-196).
- Community commitment: five ways to help each other, including fostering openness and confession (Appendix A, p.171).
- Session 20: a personal Tree Review (p.163), and goals for the next month, the next year and likely obstacles (p.164), closing with prayer requests shared in the group.

## How it could inform 2016

Setting: a woman (Vera) inside a ministry platform in 2016. The 2012 edition would have been in circulation that year. That is an inference for the fiction, not a documented fact. The rendered tree is a system instrument (operable register, the sharp side of the witness), and it is the only place the piece shows the diagnosis visually. Vera places her own relationships (drawn from her in-game conversations, not invented testimony) on one of two trees, both labelled as self-serving. No card can be placed as healthy. The programme's rhetoric is made playable and cannot be won on its own terms, while Vera's relationships are not reduced to the sort. Felt scenes stay free of any guide.
