# Research note — Avery Dame-Griff, *The Two Revolutions: A History of the Transgender Internet*
*Prepared 2026-10-01 for Sérgio and the Fable/Claude sessions. Source: the markdown copy in the Literature Review folder (Ai_socialities/Relevant/Part 1 MD). Chapter 5's youth material is the closest it comes to Daniel's world.

## 1 · The thesis, in my words (~150 words)

The title comes from a 1991 article by a trans writer who described her community as “in the midst of two revolutions” (Intro p.16): an *overt* one, the building of a shared political identity under the umbrella word transgender, and a *covert* one, the computer revolution. Dame-Griff's argument is that these were one process. Home computing, bulletin boards, commercial services, Usenet and the Web let a scattered population who had met in newsletters, hotlines and local meetings find each other, circulate vocabulary within days, publish without editors, and eventually claim a single name. The Internet was shaped by them too: platforms had to decide what counted as obscene, family-friendly or real-named because trans users were there. The costs were dependence on corporate platforms, a white and middle-class skew, weakened local groups, a youth generation that came out online and bypassed the old institutions, and, in the platform era, identity terms turned into data by tags and search. The conclusion asks for trans-owned infrastructure and preservation. (Intro pp.1–2; Concl. p.209)

## 2 · Chapter by chapter

**Introduction — Laying the Foundations (pp.1–28).** Frames the book as a *net history* that starts from users rather than inventions. Sketches the 1970s–90s movement: university clinics defunded and anti-trans radical feminism pushing groups inward by the early 1980s; Tri-Ess for heterosexual crossdressers; the Outreach Institute and Fantasia Fair; IFGE (1987) and its magazine; and the 1990s campaign to replace *gender community* with the umbrella term transgender (Holly Boswell 1991, Leslie Feinberg 1993, Tapestry renamed 1995), with genderqueer moving from a collective political umbrella to an individual identity. Describes domestic PC and Internet uptake in the 1990s and why the gender community, being heavily white-collar, was primed for it. Sets out the archive problem (BBS and AOL content vanished; the first half rests on print newsletters), the privacy rules the author follows, and the limits of the record: trans men appear online only from the late 1990s, crossdressers and trans women dominate the archive, race and class are hidden by pseudonymity. Previews the six chapters and the thesis that trans life online leaves *sediment*. *Key passages: pp.4–6, 12–13, 23, 26–28.*

**Chapter 1 — Dialing into the Revolution: The Bulletin Board System (pp.29–55).** Before BBSes, contact meant newsletters, group libraries and hotlines, each of which could out you: mail opened by the wrong person, answering machines, one caller who rang twenty-one times before a volunteer picked up. GenderNet, the first gender-community BBS, went live on 1 January 1984; by 1991 two FidoNet-style networks linked dozens of boards. The chapter follows BBSes as tools for activism (the 1989 federal ADA amendment that excluded trans people from disability protection; the US TOO network), cyberutopian rhetoric borrowed from the WELL, and daily privacy tradecraft: false-front login screens, secret keystrokes, handles, hidden and renamed files. It argues that anonymity, fast asynchronous contact and file sharing became the yardstick by which later platforms were judged, and that BBS users also reprinted online material in paper newsletters. *Key passages: pp.29–30, 34–38, 45, 47, 52–54.*

**Chapter 2 — Out of the Cybercloset, Into the Cyberstreets (pp.56–80).** Moves to the walled gardens: AOL (free trial CDs, 10 million subscribers by 1997), CompuServe, Prodigy, GEnie. Trans users made homes in each, but the services wanted a family-friendly brand and had unclear legal status. AOL's terms-of-service banned *transsexual* and *transvestite* as vulgar words, shut or privatised rooms whose names included them, and relied on volunteer guides; users answered with coded room names and then, led by the AOL Gender Group founder, a letter-writing campaign that reframed trans people as respectable, low-churn customers (one member described the chat room's talk as “a bunch of girls who'd just gone shopping”, p.71). The result was a Transgender Community Forum inside the gay-and-lesbian forum (1995), then a slow squeeze as AOL moved to flat-fee pricing and advertising logic (1996–2003); QView's print ads for the gay and lesbian forum carried the tagline “America Online's Best Kept Secret” (p.77). Argument: the community won space by speaking the platform's language (consumers, family values), a bargain that also reinforced respectability politics. *Key passages: pp.56, 65–69, 74–75, 79–80.*

**Chapter 3 — Politics and Petty Useless Bickering: Transgender Usenet and the Emergence of Cisgender (pp.81–114).** Usenet was the cross-platform, unmoderated, spam-prone network that AOL opened to its subscribers in September 1993. The chapter traces trans newsgroups (alt.transgendered, 1992; soc.support.transgendered, voted in 1994), the charters and FAQs that tried to govern them, and the long flame wars between transsexual-identified and crossdresser- or transgenderist-identified regulars. Using network analysis of the Usenet Historical Collection, it shows how one very prolific, confrontational poster pushed a performance-based definition of *cisgender* (and a four-rule *cisgender ideal*) far beyond its natural reach, thanks to quoting, cross-posting and the absence of moderation. Earlier in-group words (genetic girl, genny, natal) were biological and about realness; cisgender shifted attention to identity and performance, and only in the 2000s, once adopted by allies, narrowed from morphology to birth-assigned sex. The chapter also records the usual demographics (a 1993 poster survey, an average age near thirty-three) and Usenet's bad name among community leaders by the late 1990s. Argument: platform affordances, not consensus, determined which vocabulary spread. *Key passages: pp.83–84, 87, 91–92, 98, 113–114.*

**Chapter 4 — Always On: Information, Circulation, and the World Wide Web (pp.115–145).** Starts at AOL's flat-fee launch on 1 December 1996 and its dial-in failures. The Web replaced the labour-heavy information clearinghouse; TGForum (1995) and Remembering Our Dead (1998) are the exemplar sites. GeoCities, Tripod and AOL Hometown let individuals publish home pages, treated as ancestors of social-media profiles: non-linear, free of the transition memoir's before-and-after plot, decorated, linked into webrings and guestbooks. Creators put disclaimers on landing pages in a decade of cyberporn panic (Time 1995, the Communications Decency Act, filtering software, self-rating schemes). Disclaimers also served as a shield against chasers and a signal of good digital citizenship; one organisation's site was wrongly blocked by a library's filter and defended chiefly as respectable. The author's point is that trans advocacy, wedded to respectability, never challenged the framing that made such disclaimers necessary. *Key passages: pp.115–117, 120–123, 127–128, 136–144.*

**Chapter 5 — Becoming Obsolete in Your Own Lifetime: Membership Declines and Generation Gaps (pp.146–173).** Argues that the Internet hollowed out the local groups that had made the Internet possible. A new coming-out story begins online; BBSes and group newsletters close; elders such as a long-running Texas crossdresser couple lament that online life is fantasy and misinformation, while a Los Angeles chapter officer insists that “the Net can't replace the support group” (p.155). The second half is about youth, a group the older community treated as unthinkable because groups feared parents' lawsuits: teens found AOL, Usenet's youth newsgroup, youth nonprofits and home pages, and sometimes were outed or cut off by the family computer. Established trans bodies treated youth as vulnerable, LGB youth groups simply started trans youth groups, GenderPAC's 2000 rebrand opened a door. LiveJournal then becomes the 2000s hub, with granular privacy filters. *Key passages: pp.146–148, 152–155, 158–166, 171–173.*

**Chapter 6 — Transgender in the Platform Era (pp.174–200).** Opens in 2008 with the end of Transgender Tapestry and the rise of Tumblr. Asks how two infrastructures, social tagging and search, turn identity words into data. On Tumblr, tags are searchable identity claims, but they also produce lateral policing of who may use which tag, porn-saturated tags that users abandon, and a 2018 adult-content ban. In Google, the People Also Ask box, autocomplete and dictionary widgets return confident, decontextualised snippets about trans life (the author reads some dictionary example sentences as echoing framings common to trans-exclusionary writing), and advertisers buy identity keywords. Queer and trans users arrived on Tumblr from LiveJournal in 2008 and valued its pseudonymity and image culture; the same open tagging let strangers police definitions and let commerce flood tags with fetish content, pushing users to abandon some words. Argument: language becomes a commodity and users lose control of definitions, while the platform's corporate owner can erase a community's space overnight. *Key passages: pp.174–175, 179, 182–189, 192–200.*

**Conclusion — Owning Our History (pp.201–210).** Revisits a 2014 Time cover story that credits the Internet for trans visibility and shows what that generation-gap narrative omits. Argues that the generation-gap story leaves out the history that made a young person's first search possible. Argues for preservation: web archives cannot capture a site's layers, Flash and Yahoo Groups have closed, message boards are fragile, and corporate platforms purge what they host. Discusses a game that emulates a teen's 1999 computer as one way to recover the feel of the early Web. Proposes a cooperatively governed, member-owned trans platform with granular privacy, multiple pseudonyms, warning tags, content orphaning and an archiving plan from the start, modelled on Archive of Our Own, itself founded in 2007 after LiveJournal mass-deleted accounts that listed adult interests, and on the community-owned periodicals of earlier decades. *Key passages: pp.201–203, 205–209.*

### 4.1 · 1997 — Daniel, dial-up, the fellowship's pages, the kit, the channel, Rob's DMs

1. **A busy signal as a period detail. DOCUMENTED (ch4 p.115).** AOL went flat-fee on 1 December 1996 and customers could not get through: in one test 41% of dial-in attempts failed, 61% in the evening peak, with state attorneys-general interest by January 1997. A 1997 Internet Setup Wizard that sometimes cannot connect, or a *try again later* at the hours Daniel can use the family PC (after school, evening), is historically exact. AOL's trial CDs came in cereal boxes and with videotape rentals, and 10 million subscribers was claimed by November 1997 (ch2 p.56).
2. **The family computer is the surveillance device. DOCUMENTED (ch5 p.164).** Late-1990s trans teens' home pages were found, or the teens outed, when a parent read them; consequences included severely restricted computer and Internet access. One teen deliberately set the household browser's landing page to her coming-out letter. The shared family PC is, in effect, Daniel's whole room: a diary file, a history list and a browser start page are all things a parent could find. A start page the player sets (or that Rob/the fellowship sets) is a documented practice.
3. **Account in someone else's legal name. DOCUMENTED (ch2 pp.69–70).** AOL accounts were registered under the account holder's legal name; the book records warnings that used legal names in the body of a message to users known by screen names, read by the community as intimidation. INFERENCE: Daniel's screen name sits on his parent's account, so a mentor, a guide or a parent can see whose account it is. A moderation notice that begins *Dear [parent's name], the account holder…* would be a plausible, documented-style cruelty.
4. **Room-title rules that favour a family-safe fellowship room. DOCUMENTED (ch5 p.162; ch2 pp.65–66).** AOL did not allow public chat rooms combining words such as gay, teen and youth; the 1994 terms-of-service adviser's reasoning was that a room title containing “Yng M 4 Yng M” would be made private because the M might mean underage male (p.162), while a title announcing men seeking adults stayed public. Guides (volunteers) could convert any room to private if its name looked sexual, and the three-strike ladder ended in a six-month suspension. INFERENCE: the fellowship channel is a public, *family-friendly* room that these rules never touch, while a queer teen's own rooms are privatised or dissolved. That asymmetry is the mechanism that pushes Daniel toward the room that stays open.
5. **Filtering software and age-gates on the other side. DOCUMENTED (ch4 p.140, pp.137–138).** Filters of the mid-1990s relied on word lists, blacklists and whole-domain blocks (GeoCities and Tripod were blocked wholesale); one product, CYBERsitter, listed “Homosexuality / Transgender Sites” as a category (p.140). Many queer home pages carried a landing-page disclaimer telling anyone under 18 or 21 to go back. INFERENCE (a prop): the family's filter shows a block page when Daniel tries a gay-resources link, while the fellowship's pages load because they are labelled family or Christian; or Daniel hits a *you must be 18* landing page and a *back* button before he reaches anything useful.
6. **Self-rating labels. DOCUMENTED (ch4 pp.143–144).** RSACi (0–4 scales for nudity, sex, language, violence) and SafeSurf (which even had categories for homosexual and heterosexual themes) were metadata tags read by Internet Explorer, Netscape and filters. A GeoCities WestHollywood leader urged self-rating and suggested most sites there should be rated 15 and up. The book's argument is that rating and disclaimers conceded the premise that queer content was adult-only. INFERENCE: the kit's pages could carry a *Safe for Families* rating; the queer sites Daniel finds carry 15+ or 18+.
7. **Home-page grammar. DOCUMENTED (ch4 p.124, pp.133–134, p.136).** Period furniture: left-aligned text and tables, a visitor meter, an embedded MIDI file, guestbooks, webrings, hotlinks pages, neighbourhood addresses (GeoCities called users homesteaders; LGBT pages lived in WestHollywood), and volunteer block leaders who welcomed and reviewed new neighbours. INFERENCE: the fellowship's pages could mimic the neighbourhood-and-leader pattern (*Welcome Committee*, *Block Captain*) so that its structure looks like what the queer sites do, with a different content.
8. **Hotlines had hours. DOCUMENTED (ch4 pp.123–124; ch1 p.35).** A crossdressing club's hotline was live for two hours a week and went to an answering machine otherwise; callers often refused mailed information for fear of being outed. INFERENCE: the fellowship's phone line (if the piece has one) *staffed Tuesdays after 7*; Daniel's refusing mailed material is period-true.
9. **The mentor and the teen's own defences. ## 5 · What the book says about SOGICE, conversion practice, the religious right, and gender-critical activity online — and about trans men, the trans-masculine and butch–trans borderlands

### 5.1 · SOGICE and conversion practice

**Almost nothing.** The terms conversion therapy, reparative therapy, ex-gay, SOGICE and ministry do not occur anywhere in the text, notes, bibliography or index (I searched all of them; the only hit for exodus is users leaving BBSes). The nearest items, all of which are context, not content:

- **A Christian-owned company pressures a platform (1986).** Quantum Link (the Commodore service that became AOL) disbanded its gay/lesbian club after pressure from a Christian-owned mail-order computer firm with a financial stake in Commodore; management had also vetoed a chat room called Gay Bar (ch2 p.64; source is a 1986 gay-press report in the bibliography, p.237). This is the one example of religious pressure on an early online service. **Status for the Dossier:** documentary in the book's footnote chain, thinly sourced; label `documentary` only after Sérgio checks the 1986 article.
- **The US Senate's 1989 ADA amendment** excluded homosexuality, bisexuality, transvestism, transsexualism and gender identity disorders from disability protection; notes record that an earlier amendment by Senator Helms had already excluded transvestites (ch1 p.45; notes to ch1 n.54, p.217). A legislative expression of the political right's view of these groups, with no religious framing in the text.
- **Heritage Foundation.** A think-tank opinion piece against the informed-consent model surfaces in Google's answer box (ch6 p.191).
- **Counsellors and parents as obstacles for minors.** A physician's 1989–96 study of teenage crossdressers ran into disapproving counsellors, legal worries and about thirty cases of parents ending contact (ch5 p.159). ### 5.2 · The religious right and moral-panic politics online (what *is* there)

- **Technopanics.** Parental and political fear of children's online exposure, 1993–96: the 1993 TV Chat story (ch2 pp.66–67), the 1995 cyberporn cover story and the discredited study behind it (ch4 pp.137–138), the 1996 Communications Decency Act (ch4 p.139), filtering software (ch4 p.140), and the 1998 library-filter case (ch4 pp.141–142). ## 6 · Cautions — where the book complicates the piece's framing

1. **Scope.** The book is about trans life; Daniel (gay, 1997–2003) and Vera (lesbian, 2016) are not its subjects. Use it for infrastructure and policing, and say so in any Dossier card. The book's *youth* material includes gay and lesbian teens (ch5), but its detail is about trans teens.
2. **Platform decisions are economic in the book, not ideological.** AOL's word bans, Prodigy's screeners and the 2018 Tumblr ban are explained by brand safety, family-friendliness, churn and advertising. A scene in which a platform actively sides with a ministry cannot be backed by this book; it can only be backed by the *mechanism* of family-friendly branding. Mark any such scene as invented in the piece's own voice (CLAUDE.md: satire only inside perpetrator self-presentation).
3. **Respectability was a strategy, with costs.** The AOL campaign and the disclaimers worked by distancing the community from sex and deviance. The book treats this as effective and as limiting. The piece's respite and its felt scenes should avoid implying that respectable presentation is the moral of the story.
4. **Memoir *recipes* are not the same as testimony scripts.** The trans memoir's staged narrative was a tactical response to clinician gatekeeping, a survival practice the author treats with sympathy. A ministry's testimony script is imposed. Draw the parallel in form only, and never in value.
5. **Contagion.** The book gives many examples of isolated people finding peers online, and of elders and parents worrying about online influence and misinformation. It also argues that platform tags and search shape *categories*. A reader who wants a contagion argument could quote the second point out of context. In the piece, keep the *programme's* voice as the one that cherry-picks this kind of material; the Dossier should state that the book's argument is about classification, not causation.
6. **The community policed itself.** Elders discouraged youth-run lists as the blind leading the blind; some activists argued against transition as internalised transphobia or against alliance with gay and lesbian people; one page owner was told on Usenet to take her page down; ideology wars shaped what vocabulary spread (ch3, ch5). The book is a corrective to depicting the queer side as monolithic. This is relevant to Noa (policed from both sides) and to Vera (the queer side is not a single actor).
7. **Moderators are not only villains.** AOL's guides were mostly volunteers with little oversight, and the gay forum's contractors were paid by retention. The book describes structural pressure and individual leeway together. Vera should reflect both (and the project's own rule: never jokes at the victim).
8. **Respectability and whiteness are in the archive's frame.** The book is open that its sources overrepresent white, middle-class, US-based trans women and crossdressers; people of colour, working-class people, and non-English speakers appear mainly through absence (ch5 p.149, pp.151–152). The piece should not present the book's picture as the whole history.
9. **Anachronism risk in vocabulary.** In the 1990s cisgender was a Usenet term with a performance-based meaning; GG, genny, natal, RG, en femme/en drab, stealth, woodworking, TV/CD/TS/TG are period words (ch3 p.94; ch1 p.36). A 2003 or 2016 character should not use cis in its 2020s sense; 2016's Tumblr words are truscum, transtrender, enby, girlswholikegirls (ch3 p.91; ch6 p.181, p.198).
10. **Do not name private people.** The book anonymises teenagers and uses only first names for home pages; the piece's ethics rule on verbatim survivor testimony (ETHICS_CONSTRAINTS #2) also bars lifting the teens' diary lines. Everything in §4.1 about teen behaviour is a paraphrase of generic patterns.
11. **The book is a source for what the book says, not for facts it cites.** Many facts (the 1986 Quantum Link episode, 1994 CuD, Prodigy screener numbers) come from single press or newsletter items listed in the notes; verify with the originals before a Dossier card is marked `documentary` (CLAUDE.md: uncited items carry `[VERIFY SOURCE]` until Sérgio checks).
12. **Dates.** The copyright page says 2023; the brief says 2025 (see §0).
